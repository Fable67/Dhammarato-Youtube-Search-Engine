### Summary

Here is a summary of the Dhamma talk between Dhammarato (Speaker A) and Willie (Speaker B):

**Core Theme:** The talk focuses on the **First Jhana** (specifically the state of *satisfaction*/Sukkha) as the essential foundation for enlightenment, contrasting it with the pursuit of higher, more trance-like Jhanas.

**Key Points Made by Dhammarato:**

1.  **The "I Like It, But I Don't Want It" Mindset:** The central teaching is illustrated by a story about Ajahn Sumedho and Ajahn Chah. At a Kathin ceremony (a traditional "matchmaking" time for ex-monks), Sumedho looked at all the temptations and said, **"I like it, but I don't want it."** This is the essence of the Dhamma: the ability to find satisfaction in the present moment, which eliminates craving.

2.  **The Danger of Higher Jhanas:** The speaker explains that while higher Jhanas (2nd, 3rd, 4th) shut down the thinking mind and sensory input, they are not automatically liberating. In fact, a monk who masters these blissful states without first developing satisfaction can easily **mistake Jhanic pleasure for sensual desire** (leading to lust). These higher states are "useless" for daily life because you cannot function or investigate the mind while in them.

3.  **The Correct Order: Vipassana First, Jhana Second:** The right approach is not to climb the Jhanas and *then* do insight. The correct path is: **Vipassana (investigation) -> Satisfaction -> Jhana.** One must first learn to maintain satisfaction (First Jhana) while still being able to think and act.

4.  **The Mind's Reaction is the Trap:** Willie notes that when he briefly enters a Jhana-like state, his mind says "Yes! That's it!" and the state collapses. Dhammarato confirms this is the exact point where one must "be on guard." The "Wow!" reaction of the mind pulls you out of the state. The skill is to maintain the satisfaction without the mind getting giddy or grasping.

5.  **Enlightenment is Not Elsewhere:** The speaker argues that if you are in a state of deep satisfaction, asking "But shouldn't I be enlightened instead?" is a foolish question. **What could enlightenment be that is better than perfect satisfaction?** Enlightenment is available anytime you sit down and simply enjoy being satisfied. It is "just sitting" (Zazen) with nothing to attain.

6.  **Practical Advice for Willie:**
    - **Don't long for it:** Willie worries about losing the state. Dhammarato reassures him that if he doesn't miss it or long for it when it's gone, he hasn't really lost it. Just take a breath and come back to satisfaction.
    - **The "Emperor" Mindset:** To strengthen the quality of satisfaction, one must cultivate a sense of being a winner, a champion, or an emperor. Feeling capable and successful ("I can handle this") drives the satisfaction.
    - **Team Management:** Dhammarato advises Willie to bring donuts to his stressed team (referencing "Zen in the Art of Donuts") to relieve pressure and remind them to smile.

**Conclusion for the Student (Willie):**
Willie realizes that he has essentially been doing the practice correctly all along. The "falling apart" of his meditation was not a failure. The takeaway is to **"stay and maintain that state of satisfaction"** – to feel good now, without chasing anything else.

### Metaphors and Stories

This talk uses several powerful **metaphors** and **stories** to illustrate the core teaching about the first jhana, satisfaction, and the path to enlightenment. Here is a breakdown of the key narratives and figurative language:

### 1. The Central Metaphor: The "Satisfaction" State
- **Metaphor:** The first jhana is described not as a mystical trance, but as the **development of the skill of satisfaction**. This is a metaphor for a specific, trainable mental quality.
- **Implication:** Satisfaction is the foundation. It is a state of being "okay" with the present moment, which acts as an antidote to sensual desire and restlessness.

### 2. The Story (Metaphor) of the Kathin Ceremony & Monks Disrobing
This is the most detailed narrative in the talk. It functions as an extended metaphor for **training the mind to resist distraction and desire**.

- **The Setting:** The Kathin ceremony (end of rains retreat) is a time when young monks traditionally disrobe.
- **The Metaphor for "Good Husband":** The monks are considered good marriage material because they have been disciplined (follow orders, don't gamble, don't drink). This is a metaphor for the **disciplined, stable mind**.
- **The "Matchmaker's Paradise":** The grandmothers seeking husbands for their granddaughters represents **the world of sensual temptation and social pressure**.
- **The Twist:** A monk who has only practiced high jhanas (second, third, fourth) will be **more likely to "want it"** (get seduced) because they are used to intense pleasure. The monk who has trained in **first jhana (satisfaction)** is the one who can say: **"I like it, but I don't want it."**
- **Key Quote (Ajahn Sumedho):** *"I like it, but I don't want it."* This is framed as the "whole Dhamma" – being able to appreciate the world without being caught by craving.

### 3. The "Rosy Apple Tree" Metaphor (Reference to the Buddha)
- **Story:** The talk references the Buddha’s own account of sitting under a rose apple tree as a child and entering the first jhana (Sutta 36).
- **Implication:** This is a metaphor for **innate, non-striving awakening**. The Buddha realized that this "first jhana" state was the path, not the higher, more dramatic trances he later practiced. It was the simple, pleasurable state of satisfaction that he had forgotten.

### 4. The "Emperor / Champion" Metaphor
- **Metaphor:** To maintain satisfaction, the speaker suggests adopting an inner identity of a **"champion," "lion," or "emperor."**
- **Purpose:** This is a cognitive metaphor to generate **confidence and self-efficacy**. By saying "I am a winner," "I can do this," the practitioner builds the quality of **success** that underpins true satisfaction. It is a tool to guard against doubt and discouragement.

### 5. The Oily Bowl (A Metaphor for the Formless State)
- **Metaphor:** To explain "neither perception nor non-perception," the talk uses the image of **a clay bowl that has held oil. You can feel the oil, but you can't scoop out a drop.**
- **Implication:** This describes a state of extreme subtlety where perception is at the bare minimum – just enough to know there is consciousness, but without any content or specific perception. It is "oily" but not liquid.

### 6. The "Zen in the Art of Donuts" Metaphor
- **Metaphor:** The speaker suggests bringing donuts to a stressed team.
- **Implication:** This is a shadow metaphor for the teaching itself. The donuts (pleasure/connection) are offered not as a distraction, but as a **skillful means to bring joy and remind people of satisfaction**. It's a practical, compassionate application of "being satisfied with the way things are."

### Summary of the Core Teaching Through Metaphor:
The talk uses these stories to argue that **enlightenment is not a future goal achieved by conquering difficult states (jhanas 2-4)** . Instead, it is accessible now by **learning to maintain the simple state of satisfaction (first jhana)** . The stories of the monks, the Buddha, and the emperor are all ways to "dress up" this very simple, practical instruction: **"Stay satisfied, and you are already there."**
