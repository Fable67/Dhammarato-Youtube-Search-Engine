### Summary

This is a detailed and nuanced Dhamma discussion between **Dhammarato** and a student (Speaker A) that centers on the integration of **Jhana practice**, **the Mahasi noting method**, and the **Suttas** (specifically MN 36 and MN 119).

Here is a summary of the key points:

**1. The Problem with "Organized Methods" (like the Mahasi system)**
- Dhammarato criticizes rigid, "graded" systems (e.g., Mahasi vs. Bhikkhu Buddhadasa) for treating students as if they are in a classroom. This can leave some students behind or hold them back.
- He argues that the Mahasi method (rising/falling/noting) is actually a **westernized, incomplete version** of Anapanasati (mindfulness of breathing). Many practitioners get attached to the *labeling* without seeing that it is just a tool to get into First Jhana.

**2. The Core of the Teaching: First Jhana as the Key**
- The Buddha's enlightenment path (summarized from MN 36) begins with recollecting the **First Jhana** he experienced as a child under the rose apple tree.
- **First Jhana:** Seclusion from hindrances, accompanied by *vitakka* (applied thought) and *vicara* (sustained thought). This is not a "no-thought" state; it is the ability to **direct your mind where you want it to stay**. The pleasure (piti/sukha) comes from the relief of being free from mental wandering.

**3. The "Witness" vs. "Storyteller" Analogy**
- Dhammarato distinguishes between two types of noting:
    - **The True Witness:** Just seeing what is happening (fast, direct).
    - **The Storyteller:** Labeling and telling a story about what happened (slow, conceptual).
- **The Trap:** Heavy labeling delays perception. Successful noting (light, fast) leads *directly* into First Jhana. The goal is not to "talk about" the experience, but to **become the experience**.

**4. The "Cavalry" vs. "Failure" Mindset**
- A major theme is **attitude**. When the mind wanders and you "catch it" (mindfulness kicks in), this is a **success** (the cavalry arriving), not a failure.
- The pleasure of Jhana is born from this relief. Dhammarato emphasizes that you must **cultivate joy** ("Aha! I caught you!") rather than feeling defeated by distraction.

**5. The "Wet" vs. "Dry" Practice**
- The Buddha’s path of Jhana is "wet" (juicy, joyful).
- Practicing without that joy (dry insight) is a struggle. Dhammarato uses the analogy of bath powder (dry mind) and water (joy/success). You must "knead" the joy into the mind to unify it.
- **The Mahasi Method *can* work:** If practiced correctly, it naturally leads into Jhana. But many practitioners miss the crucial step of gladdening the mind.

**6. The Seven Steps (Sutta MN 48)**
- Dhammarato outlines the beginning of the Noble Path:
    1.  **Personal Responsibility:** Recognizing that no matter how clouded the mind is, *you* can clean it out.
    2.  **Knowledge of First Jhana:** Mastering the ability to apply and sustain attention.
    3.  **Investigation of the Four Noble Truths:** Once the mind is fit for work, you use it to investigate suffering, its origin, and the path. This is where noting becomes powerful (investigating intentions, reactions, etc.).

**7. Practical Advice for the Student**
- **Don't dismiss the body:** MN 119 describes how the rapture of Jhana pervades the body.
- **Let noting be fast:** If you cannot keep up with labeling, drop the label and just "be the witness" (the fast, direct one).
- **Make friends with thoughts:** In First Jhana, thoughts are not enemies; they are ponies you can ride for investigation.

Dhammarato confirms this, noting that the student has discovered the "juice"—the joy of catching the mind in action—which is the gateway to First Jhana.

### Metaphors

1.  **The Two Witnesses:**
    - **Metaphor:** The word "witness" has two meanings: one who simply *sees* what is happening, and one who is on the witness stand *telling a story* about what happened.
    - **Purpose:** To distinguish between bare awareness (the direct, non-conceptual seeing of reality) and the mental process of labeling or constructing a narrative about an experience. The latter, when done too strongly, creates a delay and hinders the direct perception needed for Jhana.

2.  **The Wagon Train and the Cavalry:**
    - **Metaphor:** The mind is a wagon train circled by "Indians" (hindrances, wandering thoughts). The "cavalry" arriving is the moment of mindfulness (*Sati*), recognizing the mind has wandered.
    - **Purpose:** To reframe the experience of "getting distracted" from a failure to a joyful success. The joy should be at the *arrival of mindfulness*, not sorrow at the distraction. This feeling of success generates the *rapture and pleasure* required for First Jhana.

3.  **The Bathman Kneading Bath Powder (Sutta 119):**
    - **Metaphor:** A skilled bathman sprinkles water on a metal basin of bath powder and kneads it until it is completely moist and cohesive, but not oozing.
    - **Purpose:** To illustrate how the *rapture and pleasure born of seclusion* (the "water") should be deliberately pervaded throughout the body (the "dry powder"). This unifies the mind, making it "non-dusty" and resistant to being scattered by the "wind" of hindrances.

4.  **The Bouncer Kicking a Patron Out:**
    - **Metaphor:** Getting into First Jhana is like the joy a bouncer feels when successfully ejecting a troublesome patron from a bar.
    - **Purpose:** To emphasize that the pleasure in First Jhana comes directly from the *seclusion* from hindrances. The joy is not an abstract feeling, but the active, skillful success of removing mental disturbances.

5.  **A Pony to Ride (vs. a Dangerous Thing to Chase Away):**
    - **Metaphor:** Instead of seeing a thought or hindrance as something dangerous to be eliminated, see it as a "pony you can ride."
    - **Purpose:** To change the attitude towards thoughts from aversion to investigation. It aligns with the idea that in First Jhana, thoughts are not absent; they can be applied and directed (Vitaka/Vicara) for the purpose of investigation (the Four Noble Truths).

6.  **Coming Home (after being lost in a city):**
    - **Metaphor:** Being in a state of hindrance (doubt, confusion) is like being in a new, confusing city and feeling lost. When the mind is freed from those hindrances, it is like "coming home."
    - **Purpose:** To give a visceral, relatable example of the "rapture and pleasure born of seclusion."

7.  **The Teeter-Totter (Seesaw):**
    - **Metaphor:** The spiritual path is like a teeter-totter. The first half is an uphill climb (effort, struggle), but once you reach the midpoint (the "pivot point" of Sotapanna), the teeter-totter changes and you are now running downhill (ease, fruit).
    - **Purpose:** To illustrate the difference between the *path* (active, effortful cultivation) and the *fruit* (the natural, effortless result of having seen the path).

1.  **The Buddha in the Creek (Sutta 36 - Maha-Saccaka Sutta):**
    - **Story:** The Buddha recalls his extreme asceticism. He falls into a creek and is too weak to get out. He realizes that such a weak state cannot lead to enlightenment.
    - **Purpose:** To illustrate the futility of extreme asceticism and to set up the pivotal moment of recollection that leads to the path.

2.  **The Buddha Under the Rose Apple Tree:**
    - **Story:** The Buddha recollects a childhood experience at age 12 during his father's plowing ceremony. He sat under a rose apple tree, secluded from sense desires, and entered the First Jhana.
    - **Purpose:** This is the central story of the talk. It is used to argue that the Buddha's path to enlightenment was not a new invention, but a *recollection* of a natural state of pleasure and seclusion he had already experienced.
