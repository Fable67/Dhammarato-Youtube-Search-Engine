### Summary

Here is a summary of the Dhamma talk between Dhammarato and a student (Speaker A), along with Sangha friends.

### Core Theme
The talk focuses on recognizing and dis-identifying from deeply ingrained childhood patterns and negative self-judgment. The student discusses how these old habits (feeling "not good enough," fearing judgment, perfectionism) cause stress and physical tension in their adult life and work. Dhammarato guides them to see that these feelings are not the present reality but echoes of the past.

### Key Points Discussed

1.  **The Origin of Patterns (The "Child"):**
    - Speaker A describes feeling overwhelmed by patterns of trying to be perfect, which leads to stress and fear of not being "good enough."
    - Dhammarato explains that these are old coping mechanisms formed in childhood (often from many small "traumas" rather than one big one). They are deeply ingrained habits we don't notice until we practice mindfulness (Anapanasati).

2.  **The Problem of Self-Judgment (The "Judge"):**
    - The student's fear of clients judging them is reframed. Dhammarato points out that **the real judge is inside the student's own mind**, not the clients. The student is projecting their own self-criticism onto others.
    - The student admits to reacting to this internal judgment with anger at themselves (agreeing with the "guy in your head" who says you're no good) or by "zoning out."

3.  **The Solution: Changing Attitude & Investigation:**
    - **See the Illusion:** The first step is wisdom – recognizing that there is **no logical reason** for these feelings in the *present moment*. The present is actually fine.
    - **"Down Boy" (Not Fighting):** Instead of fighting the feelings (which gives them energy) or being run over by them, Dhammarato suggests a gentle, commanding attitude: "Down boy." Treat the feeling like a barking dog that is barking at nothing real. Don't bark back.
    - **Investigate the Feeling:** Look at the feeling directly. Where is it in the body? Can you breathe into it? Can you make it bigger or smaller? This turns the feeling from a "boss" into a "toy" or a "pet" you can observe.
    - **Dis-Identification (Anatta):** The core teaching is **"You are not that feeling."** By investigating, you realize the feeling is an object, not your identity. You are the one *seeing* the feeling, not the feeling itself.

4.  **Practical Advice:**
    - **Breath as an Anchor:** When overwhelmed, take a deep breath. The state of tension is often linked to not breathing well.
    - **The "Jack-in-the-Box":** These old feelings will pop up. The skill is to calmly put them back in the box, recognizing their nature.
    - **Gentle Care vs. Harsh Anger:** The student shares a breakthrough: when they stopped being angry at the "vulnerable child" part of themselves and instead showed it compassion, the anxiety quickly subsided. Dhammarato confirms this is the "healthy adult" or "noble" part of the mind taking care of the other parts.

5.  **Key Metaphors:**
    - **The Barking Dog:** Don't bark back at the feeling (anger/aggression), just calm it down.
    - **The Mack Truck:** Don't try to stop the truck (forceful control) or let it run you over (passive victim). Just step aside and watch it pass.
    - **The Emperor on a Pile of Dirt:** Instead of being buried by your mental dirt (hindrances), sit on top of it and be in charge.
    - **"Aha, I see you, Mara":** This is the Buddha's own practice of recognizing the defilements (Mara) clearly, which is the act of waking up (Sati).

### Conclusion
Dhammarato congratulates the student on their progress, noting they are seeing these deep patterns "face to face" for the first time. The student realizes the practice is not about brute force concentration (as in *The Mind Illuminated*), but about understanding and investigating the hindrances with a change in attitude. The final advice is to develop the skill of **Sati (wakefulness)** – remembering to "wake up" and see the feelings for what they are, over and over again.

### Metaphors and Stories

This conversation is rich with metaphors and stories used to illustrate psychological patterns and the practice of mindfulness (Anapanasati). Here is a breakdown of the key metaphors and stories used by Speaker B (the teacher/guide):

### Central Metaphors

1.  **The Map and the Territory**
    - **Usage:** Speaker B uses this from NLP to explain childhood development. Children create internal "maps" of the world to cope with being overwhelmed. These maps become outdated habits that interfere with adult life.
    - **Quote:** *“This is where NLP talks about in the sense of map making, which fits perfectly into the teachings of the Buddha…”*

2.  **The Internal Judge / Courtroom**
    - **Usage:** To reframe Speaker A's fear of being judged by clients. The metaphor reveals that the real judge is internal (the "guys in your head"), and Speaker A is projecting that judge onto others, creating a "one-down" (inferior) position for themselves.
    - **Quote:** *“The only judge that you've got is the same judge that they have, and that's themselves.”*

3.  **The Jack-in-the-Box**
    - **Usage:** Describes how old, powerful feelings (like inadequacy) will inevitably pop up, no matter how slowly or carefully you try to suppress them. The point is not to prevent the pop, but to put the feeling “back in its box” without reacting.
    - **Quote:** *“It comes up like a jack-in-the-box. … Our job is just to put it back in his box and close the lid…”*

4.  **The Barking Dog**
    - **Usage:** A primary metaphor for dealing with difficult thoughts and feelings. If you yell at the dog (your own anxiety), you validate the threat and make it worse. The solution is a calm, loving, authoritative "Down, boy."
    - **Quote:** *“You’re the barking dog, and then when we bark at the barking dog, that it goes all over the place.”* (And later: *“Down, boy. Sit, be quiet.”*)

5.  **The Emperor and the Pile of Dirt**
    - **Usage:** A metaphor for the human condition. Most people are buried under their mental habits ("pile of dirt"). The goal of practice is to become the "emperor" who sits *on top* of the pile, in control.
    - **Quote:** *“That each one of us is an emperor of our own pile of dirt. … Most people are buried under their own pile of dirt. … Let's come out from under that pile of dirt and sit on top of it.”*

6.  **Digging a Grave**
    - **Usage:** A dark but powerful metaphor for the process of self-investigation. As you get better at seeing your patterns, you dig "deeper graves." This is framed positively: it is the "grave of the self" (the ego/identity that clings to these feelings).
    - **Quote:** *“This is the grave of the self. … The grave of the one who clings to these feelings.”*

7.  **The Mack Truck (or Lorry)**
    - **Usage:** A metaphor for a powerful, overwhelming thought or feeling bearing down on you. You have three choices: 1) Get run over (victim), 2) Try to stop it with brute force (futile effort), or 3) **Step out of the way** (equanimity/wisdom).
    - **Quote:** *“The third option is merely stand out of the way. … We merely want to stand out of its way so that we can take a look at it while it's passing by.”*

Speaker B helps her trace this back to a childhood wound (multiple small "traumas" rather than one big one). This story is used to introduce the idea that feelings are old, outdated maps.

2.  **The Story of Jesus in the Temple**
    - **Usage:** To illustrate that doing the "right thing" (acting with a noble mind) can have dangerous consequences. It's a cautionary tale to use wisdom and not just brute force.
    - **Quote:** *“Jesus walked into the temple, threw out the money changers, and within a week they had been nailed up. He did the right thing. But the point that he made ticked a whole lot of people off.”*

6.  **The "Mom and Kitty" Family Story**
