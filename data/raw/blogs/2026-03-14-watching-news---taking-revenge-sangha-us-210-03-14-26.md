### Summary

Here is a summary of the Dhamma talk with Dhammarato and Sangha friends.

**Core Theme:** The talk explores the concept of **judgment** as the root of suffering, using the Biblical story of Adam and Eve as a central metaphor. Dhammarato reinterprets the "fruit of knowledge of good and evil" not as a literal apple, but as the human tendency to judge everything as "good" or "bad," which leads to dissatisfaction and the loss of our natural "paradise."

**Key Points:**

1.  **The Problem of Judgment:** Humans have an innate drive to "know" how to feel about things, stemming from a survival instinct that prioritizes danger. This leads us to constantly label experiences as pleasant or unpleasant. Judging things as "good" creates clinging, while judging them as "bad" creates aversion and a desire to repel or seek revenge.

2.  **Revenge as the Engine of Suffering:** Dhammarato argues that much of human suffering is driven by a desire for **revenge** (often disguised as "justice"). We hold onto memories of being wronged, and every time we recall these memories, we re-experience the pain and strengthen our desire for retribution. This is a "bucket of shit" we carry around.

3.  **The Self as a Repository:** The "self" is described as the house where we store all our grievances and desires for revenge. To be free, we must let go of this need to "get even" or "get our shit back."

4.  **The Role of the News:** Media (TV news, social media algorithms) are in the business of making people unhappy and angry. They feed our desire for revenge by constantly presenting us with enemies and bad news, which keeps us agitated and ensures we keep consuming their content.

5.  **The Solution: Forget and Let Go:** The primary practice recommended is **active forgetfulness**—not holding onto memories of being wronged. Instead of forgiving (which still involves remembering the offense), Dhammarato advises to simply "forget all about it." The key is to **"not take it personally"** and drop the thought of revenge as soon as it arises.

6.  **The Result: Freedom and Peace:** Letting go of revenge and judgment leads to a life that is "easy," "chill," and "peaceful." It creates space for freedom, joy, and the ability to live in the present moment rather than being trapped in a painful past. The ultimate goal is to become "empty of revenge," which is described as a supreme relief.

**Memorable Quotes & Analogies:**
- **"Adam and Eve didn't eat a fruit; they had to put up with the results of their actions... they went around paradise saying, 'I like that. I don't like that.'"** (Judgment is the original sin).
- **"The self is that structure, that house that we keep all of our crap in... designed for revenge."**
- **"Call the whole thing off."** (Advice for dealing with grudges).
- **"Freedom's just another word for nothing left to lose... all we really had to lose anyway was just the revenge."** (Quoting a song to sum up the talk's message).

**In short:** To find peace, stop judging experiences as good or bad, and stop seeking revenge for past wrongs. The path to happiness is to actively forget grievances and drop the personal investment in being a "target."

### Metaphors and Stories

This talk by Dhammarato uses a central extended metaphor along with several supporting analogies and stories to explore the theme of judgment, revenge, and suffering. Here is a breakdown of the metaphors and stories used:

### 1. The Central Extended Metaphor: The Garden of Eden / The Story of Adam and Eve
This is the primary story used to frame the entire talk. Dhammarato offers a non-literal, Dhamma-oriented reinterpretation of the Biblical story.

- **The Metaphor:** The Garden of Eden is a metaphor for **the natural, peaceful state of being (Nibbana or a mind free from judgment).** Adam and Eve are metaphors for **humanity (both male and female).**
- **"The Fruit"** is not a literal apple but a metaphor for **"the results of one's actions" (Kamma Phala).** He gives examples like "fruit of the loin" (children) and "fruit of your labors" (paycheck).
- **"The Fruit of the Knowledge of Good and Evil"** is a metaphor for **the act of judging.** Eating this fruit means going around paradise (the present moment) and labeling everything as "good" or "bad," "I like this" or "I don't like that."
- **The Result:** Adam and Eve didn't get kicked out by an angry God. They **kicked themselves out** of the peaceful state by their own act of judging. The "curse" is the inevitable suffering (dukkha) that comes from making these judgments.

### 2. Supporting Metaphors for the Self and Revenge

- **The Self is a "House" for "Crap" (or "Shit"):**
    - **Metaphor:** The "self" (the ego) is compared to a **house or a structure**. The sole purpose of this structure is to hold all of our accumulated resentments and desires for revenge.
    - **The "Crap" (or "Shit"):** This refers to the stored-up memories of being wronged, insults, traumas, and grudges. This "crap" is the fuel for our revenge.
    - **Action:** The goal is to have a "leaky" house or a "leaky" revenge bucket. When the house is empty of this crap, there is nothing left of the self that wants revenge.

- **The "Revenge Bucket":**
    - **Metaphor:** We each carry an internal **bucket** where we collect all the "bad stuff" (insults, injuries, injustices). The purpose of this bucket is to eventually "sling" the shit back at the person who wronged us.
    - **The Futility:** The speaker notes, "you can't sling some shit without it going on yourself." This highlights the self-destructive nature of holding onto revenge.

- **The Self is a "Target" for "Stray Bullets":**
    - **Metaphor:** Life is a battlefield with "stray bullets" (random insults, perceived slights, hurts from others). The "self" is a **moving target** that deliberately positions itself to get hit.
    - **Action:** We do this so we can have a reason to feel wronged and therefore have a justification for seeking revenge. The craving is for the grievance itself, not for peace.

### 3. Analogies and Smaller Stories

- **The Spanking Child:**
    - **Story:** A child is spanked once. The child remembers the event and, by thinking about it over and over, makes the memory worse and worse. The feeling of "injury" grows far beyond the original physical sensation.
    - **Point:** This illustrates how we actively create and amplify our suffering by ruminating on past hurts. The memory is not a static record but a dynamic act of creation.

- **The Cop Giving a Ticket:**
    - **Story:** A police officer gives out a dozen tickets a day. He doesn't remember any of them. The driver who gets a single ticket will remember it for years.
    - **Point:** The person who feels wronged takes it "personally," while the "perpetrator" often has no memory of the event. This shows that the suffering is generated from within, not by the external event.

- **The Monk's Forgiveness:**
    - **Story:** A young monk insults a senior monk. The senior monk forgets about it. The young monk holds onto the guilt. His friend tells him to apologize. When he does, the senior monk says, "Oh, I forgot all about it. It's not an issue."
    - **Point:** This is contrasted with the common human tendency to hold grudges for years. The ideal is to be like the senior monk and simply forget.

- **The "Senior Monk" and the Disciple:**
    - **Story (within the dialogue):** A senior monk (Ajahn Mahaboa) is referenced saying, "the world is a toilet full of shit."
    - **Metaphor:** The world is a toilet full of shit.
    - **Point:** The correct response is not to try and "clean the toilet" (fix the world, get justice/revenge). The correct response is to recognize that the *only* shit that needs cleaning is the "shit" you are carrying inside yourself. Leave the world alone.

- **"Freedom's Just Another Word for Nothing Left to Lose":**
    - **Reference:** This is a quote from the song "Me and Bobby McGee" by Kris Kristofferson, famously sung by Janis Joplin.
    - **Metaphor:** Freedom is the state of having "nothing left to lose," which in this context means having no more revenge, no more grudges, no more "crap" in the self-house. It is a state of emptiness, relief, and supreme ease.

In summary, the talk uses the **Adam and Eve story** as its central allegory for the origin of suffering through judgment, and then uses a series of **vulgar, vivid, and memorable metaphors (the house of crap, the revenge bucket, the target for bullets)** to explain how we construct a "self" specifically to hold onto these grievances and maintain a state of seeking revenge.
