### Summary

This Dhamma talk, led by Dhammarato (Speaker A) in dialogue with a Sangha friend (Speaker B), explores the practice of **standing meditation** as a profound tool for working with anxiety, restlessness, and dissatisfaction. Here is a concise summary of the key teachings:

### **Core Theme: Using Standing Meditation to Meet Anxiety**
The talk uses the simple, everyday act of "waiting" (for a bus, a doctor, an appointment) as a universal example of how we generate suffering. The physical fidgeting and agitation we display while waiting are external symptoms of an internal state of **wanting**—wanting the future moment to arrive, wanting something we don't have now.

### **Key Points:**

1.  **Standing Meditation as Training:** The practice is to stand still, without fidgeting, using the posture itself as an object of mindfulness. This trains "restraint," not through force, but by bringing awareness to the agitation. Examples given are soldiers at attention or the Buckingham Palace guards.

2.  **The Root of Agitation is "Wanting":** Restlessness, boredom, and anxiety all stem from the same source: **dissatisfaction with the present moment**. We mentally construct a desired reality (e.g., "the bus being here") and then suffer because reality doesn't match our projection. The mind adds something that isn't there, then feels bad about its absence.

3.  **Anxiety as a Bodily Experience:** The feeling of anxiety or emptiness is framed as a set of temporary bodily chemicals or sensations, not an inherent truth or personal flaw. The problem isn't the sensation itself, but our **aversion** to it—our desperate need to make it go away, which often leads to unskillful actions (like mindlessly shopping or frantically checking email).

4.  **Changing Our Relationship to Anxiety:** Instead of trying to eliminate the feeling, the Dhamma practice is to:
    *   **Investigate it playfully:** See it as a "toy" or a teacher. Use the breath to explore it—can you make it grow or shrink with the in-breath and out-breath? Can you move the sensation?
    *   **Befriend it:** Shift from "I hate this, go away" to "Ah, this is interesting. Welcome." This is a shift from an unwholesome to a wholesome mental relationship.
    *   **See the Relief in the Present:** Recognize that in the moment of waiting, **there is actually nothing to do**. The bus isn't here, so you can relax. The relief we seek in the future is available now by letting go of the wanting.

5.  **Social & Psychological Context:** The talk delves into how our society and even propaganda machines (referencing Edward Bernays) expertly manipulate this underlying anxiety to control behavior (e.g., through political ads or marketing). Much of human activity is driven by ignorant reactions to this free-floating anxiety.

6.  **Connection to Deeper Practice:** This work is directly linked to:
    *   **The Four Noble Truths:** Suffering arises from craving; cessation is possible here and now.
    *   **Dependent Origination (*Paṭiccasamuppāda*):** We create our own mental "sense sphere" (*salāyatana*) of suffering by how we attend to sense contact.
    *   **Jhāna & Peace:** By focusing on the cessation of suffering (the Third Noble Truth) and being okay with the present, the mind naturally settles into peace.

### **Practical Takeaways:**
*   Use daily moments of waiting as opportunities for **"standing meditation."**
*   When agitation arises, **don't chase the future** (the bus, the email). Instead, **come back to the body** and its sensations.
*   **Reframe anxiety** from a threat to an object of curious, friendly investigation.
*   **Recognize the mental construction:** Ask, "What am I adding to this moment that is causing me to want something else?"
*   **Find the safety and completeness in the present**, independent of external conditions.

In essence, the talk teaches that **standing still physically is a training ground for learning to be mentally still amidst the wanting mind.** By doing so, we stop creating suffering and begin to live in the "real world"—the direct, unfiltered experience of the present—which is "marvelous enough right on its own."

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the detailed transcript, here are the key **metaphors and stories** used in the talk to illustrate the teachings on restlessness, anxiety, and meditation:

### **Metaphors**

1.  **The Hole (or God-Shaped Hole):** Used to describe the feeling of emptiness, lack, or dissatisfaction that people try to fill with external things—possessions, relationships, status, or even religious belief. The speaker clarifies that the hole isn't a fixed shape waiting for a specific filler (like God); rather, we try to force whatever we crave into it, which only creates a "junky hole" full of temporary fixes.

2.  **Anxiety/Restlessness as a Toy:** Instead of viewing anxiety as an enemy to eliminate, the speaker suggests reframing it as a "toy" or "friend" to investigate playfully. One can "breathe into it," observe if it grows or shrinks, moves or changes, thereby dis-identifying from the aversion to it.

3.  **The Barking Sheepdog and the Herd:** This metaphor describes societal and political manipulation. Propaganda (the barking) induces fear/anxiety in the public (the herd), which then herds together and follows instructions (vote, donate, etc.) in a misguided attempt to feel safe and relieve that anxiety.

4.  **Adding the Bus to the Street:** A central metaphor for how the mind creates suffering. While waiting for a bus, the present-moment reality is "no bus." The mind, however, adds the *idea* of the bus and the *wanting* of the bus to the scene. This mental addition creates the agitation, not the actual situation.

5.  **Body Chemistry / Chemicals:** A recurring metaphor for feelings and emotions (anxiety, joy, fear). Framing them as impermanent bodily chemicals makes them seem less personal, solid, and threatening, and more as transient phenomena to be observed.

### **Stories & Anecdotes**

1.  **The Palace Guards at Buckingham Palace:** Used as an example of disciplined, still standing. They embody "standing at attention"—fully present and unmoved by external provocations (like tourists trying to make them laugh). This is held up as an ideal for "standing meditation" in daily life.

2.  **The Man Mending the Fence at Night:** A parable about free-floating anxiety. A man feels anxious, convinces himself the cause is a broken fence, and goes out to mend it. The physical work distracts him, and the anxiety subsides. When he returns to bed, the anxiety returns, revealing its true nature: a habitual internal state, not caused by external circumstances. The story illustrates how much "needless work" is driven by the ignorant reaction to anxiety.

3.  **Waiting for Ajahn Po:** A personal anecdote from Speaker A. While others fidgeted impatiently waiting to speak to a teacher, the speaker used the time as "standing meditation," not as "waiting." This shift in perspective—from wanting a future event to being fully in the present posture—transformed the experience from agitation to practice.

4.  **Children on a Long Car Journey:** Illustrates boredom and restlessness as forms of dissatisfaction with the present. The children are not living in the reality of the comfortable back seat; they are mentally adding their desired destination to the present, which creates impatience.

5.  **Edward Bernays and Women Smoking:** A historical story about the origins of modern propaganda. Bernays, a nephew of Freud, used psychological principles to create anxiety (about social taboos) and then link a product (cigarettes) to liberation from that anxiety, thereby manipulating public behavior. This story grounds the "sheepdog" metaphor in a real-world example.

6.  **The Email about Political Donations:** A modern, mundane example of the anxiety-manipulation cycle. An email induces anxiety about a political outcome ("If you don't act, the bad guys will win!") and immediately offers a solution that relieves the feeling ("Send $5"). This illustrates how the pattern plays out in everyday life.

**In summary,** the talk uses relatable metaphors (the hole, the missing bus, body chemicals) and vivid stories (the fence-mender, the palace guards, the propaganda pioneer) to concretely explain how anxiety arises from mental projection and craving, and how mindfulness and a shift in perspective can allow one to "be here now" without agitation.
