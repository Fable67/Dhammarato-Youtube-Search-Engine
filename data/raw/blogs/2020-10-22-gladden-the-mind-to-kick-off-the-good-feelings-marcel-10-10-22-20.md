### Summary

This Dhamma talk, led by Dhammarato (Speaker A) with a student (Speaker B, Marcel), focuses on deepening meditation practice by moving from active verbal thinking to a more direct, experiential awareness, ultimately pointing toward the first and second *jhanas* (meditative absorptions).

Here is a summary of the key points:

### **Core Theme: From Verbal Thinking to Direct Experience**
The central instruction is to move beyond the constant internal narration ("the sports announcer" in the mind) and learn to experience sensory reality directly, without the filter of conceptual storytelling.

### **Key Insights and Instructions:**

1.  **Thought is Multi-Dimensional:** Thought isn't just verbal. It includes visual imagery, emotional feeling, and memory. The initial goal in meditation is not to stop all thinking, but first to quiet the **verbal dialogue**.

2.  **The "Wholesome Tree" Analogy:** The mind is like a monkey jumping between trees (thoughts). The practice is to train it to jump only in the "wholesome tree" of present-moment sensory awareness (e.g., the breath, bodily sensations, sounds) and to abandon unwholesome thoughts (past/future worries, negative stories).

3.  **Path of the Jhanas:**
    *   **First Jhana:** Achieved by "talking yourself into" a state of well-being (*piti* - rapture/joy and *sukkha* - pleasure) using wholesome verbal guidance (e.g., "I breathe in joy, I breathe out relaxation"). The mind is collected and focused, but internal verbal dialogue is still present, though simplified.
    *   **Transition to Second Jhana:** Once a strong, joyful feeling is established (like "goosebumps"), you can let go of the verbal crutch. Dhammarato uses the metaphor: *"Once we kick the football into the air, the football don't need no leg."* You stop describing the good feeling and just fully experience it.
    *   **Second Jhana:** Characterized by the subsiding of directed thought and evaluation (*vitakka-vicara*). The internal verbal narrative ceases, leaving a deeper, wordless experience of joy and pleasure born of concentration. **Crucially, other forms of "thinking" (like subtle visual/emotional awareness) remain.**

4.  **Practical Techniques:**
    *   **Bare Knowing:** Shift from noting *with language* ("my head is nodding") to a bare, non-verbal awareness of the sensation itself.
    *   **The "Buddho" Mantra:** Use the mantra "Buddho" synchronised with breath ('Bu' on in-breath, 'dho' on out-breath) to corral verbal thought into a single, simple point of focus. The emphasis is on elongating the out-breath and resting in the silent gap after exhalation.
    *   **Skillful "Aha!":** When you notice the mind wandering, use a light, non-judgmental "Aha, I see you" to redirect attention back to the wholesome sensory present, without frustration.

5.  **Attitude is Everything:**
    *   **Don't Force It:** Making silence a goal or a job creates tension. It should feel "easy peasy"—a letting go rather than an effortful doing.
    *   **Embrace Surprise:** The mind's true quiet often comes as a surprise. The trick is to not immediately think *"My mind is quiet!"* and thus lose the silence.
    *   **Acceptance Over Analysis:** When pleasant or unpleasant sensations arise (like doubt or a facial tension), don't analyze their cause. Instead, adopt the attitude: *"Is it okay that this is here?"* Be the satisfied "king" who can allow minor irritations ("bandits") to exist without being disturbed by them.

### **Conclusion for the Student (Marcel):**
Dhammarato encourages Marcel to continue practicing by:
1.  Using verbal guidance to establish joy and satisfaction.
2.  Then letting go of the inner talk to directly experience that joy.
3.  Always returning the "monkey mind" to the wholesome tree of **present-moment sensory input** (tactile feelings, breath, posture).
4.  Cultivating an attitude of acceptance and satisfaction with whatever arises, seeing it as a challenge that proves his growing stability.

The ultimate aim is not to become inert, but to develop the skill to access profound peace and joy at will, and eventually to integrate that stable, joyful awareness into all activities, even with eyes open.

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the transcript, Speaker A uses several key metaphors and stories to explain meditation concepts, particularly related to Jhana states and quieting the mind:

**Metaphors:**

1.  **The Football (Soccer Ball) Kick:** This is the central metaphor. The "leg" that kicks the football represents **verbal thought** (the internal narrator). The "football in the air" represents the **state of joyful feeling (Piti/Sukkha)**. Once the feeling is established ("the ball is in the air"), you no longer need the verbal thought ("the leg") to sustain it. The goal is to enjoy the flight of the ball without talking about it.

2.  **The Monkey Mind:** A common meditation metaphor. Here, it's specified that the goal is not to stop the monkey, but to **train it to jump only in the "wholesome tree."** This tree represents wholesome, present-moment sensory awareness (body, breath, sight, sound) instead of unwholesome past/future stories.

3.  **The Sports Announcer / Running Radio Broadcast:** This describes the constant **internal verbal dialogue** that narrates our experience. Like an old-time radio announcer trying to describe a fast boxing match, this narration is incomplete and actually interferes with directly experiencing the event (our present-moment reality).

4.  **The Thorn:** Used to explain using thought to end thought. Just as a doctor (or a story about a lion and a rat) uses a second thorn to remove the first thorn stuck in a paw, we use **wholesome verbal guidance** (like "relax" or "aha, I see you, thought") to remove unwholesome, habitual thinking.

5.  **The Book Cover:** To explain "bare awareness," the speaker references the saying "you can't judge a book by its cover." In meditation, we are to be interested **only in the "cover"**—the bare sensory input (sight, sound, sensation)—not the "story inside" (the mental commentary, labels, and narratives about it).

6.  **The King and the Bandit:** Used to address dealing with distractions like an itch or pain. The meditator is the **king**, and the annoyance is a **bandit**. The skillful attitude is knowing the king has the power to deal with the bandit but can choose to confidently let him be for now, without being annoyed or ruled by him. This leads to relaxation.

**Stories / Analogies:**

1.  **The Carpenter's Visualization:** A story to illustrate **non-verbal thought**. A carpenter knows what his table will look like not through words, but through a **pictorial, visual thought**. This distinguishes visual thinking from verbal thinking.

2.  **The 1940s Radio Announcer:** This extends the sports announcer metaphor into a mini-story about the transition from radio to TV, illustrating how our mind's habit of verbal narration is an outdated holdover that obscures direct experience.

3.  **The Danger of Second Jhana (Morgue/ICU):** A cautionary tale. If one were to get stuck in the non-verbal, deeply absorbed state of second Jhana, one might not respond to the physical world—potentially ending up in the morgue while still feeling bliss! This story emphasizes that these states are "toys to play with," not permanent abodes, and must be practiced safely.

4.  **The Lion and the Thorn:** A brief reference to a traditional story (likely from the Jataka tales or similar folklore) to illustrate the principle of using a tool (wholesome thought) to remove a problem (unwholesome thought).

**In summary,** the speaker uses the **football kick** as the core metaphor for transitioning from first to second Jhana by dropping verbal narration. The **monkey mind** and **sports announcer** metaphors describe the problem of mental chatter. The **thorn** and **king/bandit** metaphors offer strategies for dealing with it. The **carpenter** and **book cover** analogies help distinguish between types of thinking and awareness. The **morgue story** serves as a vivid warning about the power and proper context of deep meditative states.
