### Summary

Here is a summary of the Dhamma talk between Dhammarato (Speaker A) and Wayne (Speaker B):

**Core Theme:** The talk focuses on returning to Buddhist practice after burnout, emphasizing a shift from striving and goal-oriented meditation to a joyful, sustainable, and immediate practice.

**Key Points Discussed:**

1.  **The Problem with Western Striving:** Wayne (the student) explains he burned out from meditation by striving too hard (meditating 5-6 hours a day) and wanting to be somewhere he wasn't. Dhammarato validates this, noting that Westerners often try to hit a brick wall with effort.

2.  **The Core Skill: Sati (Mindfulness/Remembering):** The central teaching is that mindfulness (Sati) is the key skill. It's not about achieving a distant goal (like Jhana or enlightenment) but about **remembering** to be present right now. Dhammarato uses the analogy of training a dog: when the mind wanders, you "catch it" and reward it with joy, rather than scolding yourself.

3.  **Right Effort = Least Effort:** Dhammarato redefines Right Effort as "the least amount of effort necessary to get the job done." The "Aha! I caught you" moment when noticing a wandering mind *is* the effort; it should be accompanied by joy, not frustration.

4.  **Moving from Victim to Lion:** He contrasts living as a victim (driven by fear, greed, and ill will, which are instinctual/reptilian brain functions) with living as a "lion" or a winner—choosing to be satisfied and joyful right now.

5.  **Direct Access to Joy:** Wayne struggles with trying to cultivate joy, only to have negative emotions arise. Dhammarato advises him to **go directly to joy** ("B") without needing a precondition ("A"). He suggests giving yourself permission to be joyful intentionally (e.g., thinking of silly songs, good memories) to change old habits of feeling bad.

6.  **Working with the Reptilian Brain:** The instincts (anxiety, restlessness, fear) are natural survival mechanisms. The practice is not to destroy them but to thank them for their service and then let the "wise one" (frontal cortex) take over.

7.  **The Role of the Breath:** Deep, diaphragmatic breathing is the "step 1" of Anapanasati. It oxygenates the frontal cortex, brings you to the present moment, and allows you to relax.

8.  **The GREB Critique:** Dhammarato critiques the influences of Government, Religion, Education, and Big Business (GREB) for teaching people they "need" things they don't, promoting fear, and reinforcing a victim mentality.

9.  **Practical Instruction:**
    - When the mind wanders, say: "Aha! I caught you."
    - Take a deep, joyful breath, relax, and enjoy the moment.
    - Watch the body and sensations without judgment.
    - Adopt the attitude: "Never mind, start again."

**Closing Advice:** Dhammarato encourages Wayne to practice with a **can-do, joyful attitude** and to remember that the Dhamma is "good in the beginning, good in the middle, and good in the end." He suggests Wayne try this approach for a week and call back to report.

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the talk, several metaphors and stories are used to illustrate Buddhist concepts, particularly around mindfulness (Sati), effort, and attitude. Here’s a breakdown:

### Key Metaphors

1.  **The Car Hitting a Brick Wall:**
    - **Used for:** Over-exertion and burnout in meditation.
    - **Quote:** "If you take your automobile and you hit it as hard and as fast as you can right into a brick wall, you won't drive for a little while."
    - **Meaning:** Meditation is not about force; unsustainable effort leads to a crash.

2.  **The Dog Training (Puppy):**
    - **Used for:** Training the mind (Sati) to return to the present moment.
    - **Quote:** "It's very much like training a dog to come when it's called... you should come when he is called... He'll get some reward."
    - **Meaning:** Instead of scolding yourself when the mind wanders, you should "pet the puppy" (reward yourself with joy) when you notice the wandering and return to the breath.

3.  **The Supercomputer vs. the Laptop:**
    - **Used for:** The function of the human brain (frontal cortex vs. reptilian brain).
    - **Quote:** "This frontal lobe is very much like a supercomputer. And the reptilian brain is more like a laptop and can run on a laptop's juice... But a supercomputer requires a super amount of electricity."
    - **Meaning:** The mindful, wise state (frontal cortex) requires energy (oxygen from deep breathing), while the instinctual, reactive state (reptilian brain) runs on low power.

4.  **The Bucket (Vessel of Self):**
    - **Used for:** The concept of self (ego) as the container for suffering.
    - **Quote:** "You can think of the self as the bucket that suffering is carried in... without that vessel of a self, who is it that's going to receive the suffering?"
    - **Meaning:** Suffering arises because there is a "self" to experience it. Letting go of the self eliminates the recipient of suffering.

5.  **The Strange Bridge:**
    - **Used for:** The futility of materialism and excess beyond basic needs (requisites).
    - **Quote:** "We built a really fine bridge to cross this river, now let's build another bridge on top of that to make it super special... See how stupid that whole idea is?"
    - **Meaning:** Once you have enough (the floor), more of the same does not increase happiness; it is just redundant accumulation.

6.  **The Lion vs. The Victim:**
    - **Used for:** Right attitude vs. wrong attitude in practice.
    - **Quote:** "You're a winner, you're a lion... Most of the time we spend our lives as a victim."
    - **Meaning:** A "victim" approaches meditation with doubt, fear, and striving. A "lion" (or winner) approaches it with confidence, joy, and the ability to "catch" the mind without self-judgment.

### Key Stories & Anecdotes

1.  **Bhikkhu Buddhadasa's Saying:**
    - **Story:** Speaker A mentions discussing the phrase "If at first you don't succeed, try, try again" with Bhikkhu Buddhadasa.
    - **The twist:** "He laughed and says, oh no, if at first you don't succeed, look at what you're doing."
    - **Purpose:** To critique the Western, competitive approach to meditation. The problem is not lack of effort, but wrong method or attitude.

2.  **The Murphy’s Law of the Hotel Computer:**
    - **Story:** A hotel computer system fails only when the hotel is full (the worst possible moment).
    - **Analogy:** "The human mind is very much like that. When we get overloaded, then we revert back to an old primitive method of dealing with things... called instincts."
    - **Purpose:** To explain why people fall back into reactivity when stressed; the "reptilian brain" takes over.

3.  **The Dusting Yourself Off:**
    - **Story:** Referencing the saying "picking yourself up, dusting yourself off, and starting all over again," attributed to Goenka ("Never mind, start again").
    - **Purpose:** To illustrate the resilient, non-judgmental attitude required for mindfulness practice. Failure is just an opportunity to begin again.

4.  **The Bible & Gloria Steinem Quote:**
    - **Story:** A reference to John 8:32 ("Ye shall know the truth, and the truth shall set you free") with a modern addendum.
    - **Quote:** "But first it'll piss you off."
    - **Purpose:** To normalize the frustration that arises when you see your own mental habits (greed, anger, delusion) clearly for the first time. The irritation is a sign of awakening, not a problem.

### Summary of Application

The speaker uses these metaphors to shift the listener (Wayne) from a **"striving/victim"** mindset (where meditation is hard work to achieve a distant goal) to a **"lion/winner"** mindset (where meditation is a playful, immediate practice of catching the mind, rewarding it with joy, and starting again).
