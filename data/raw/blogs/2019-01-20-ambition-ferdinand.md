### Summary

Here is a summary of the Dhamma talk between Dhammarato and a student (referred to as "Speaker A"):

**Core Theme:** The student discusses his progress in mindfulness and moving away from restlessness and the "victim" mindset. The conversation then pivots to a deep exploration of **goals, ambition, and the nature of desire on the Noble Path.**

**Key Points of the Discussion:**

1.  **The Paradox of Goals:** The student asks about setting goals. Dhammarato explains that the long-term aim of the path is **goallessness**—a state of complete satisfaction without needing to achieve anything external. However, he warns against the trap of "desiring desirelessness" or "wanting enlightenment," as that is itself suffering.

2.  **Small, Skillful Intentions vs. Grandiose Ambition:** Instead of big, ego-driven goals (like "saving the world"), Dhammarato advocates for **tiny, achievable intentions** that are done skillfully and joyfully. He gives the example of a trip to Bangkok where his only goal was to **smile at everyone**—a practical, present-moment application of Metta (loving-kindness) that creates safety and connection.

3.  **Replacing Desire with Skill:** Progress is not about striving for more, but about **deflating desire** and replacing it with developed **skill**. True happiness and peace are found internally, not by chasing results in the outside world.

4.  **Guarding Your Joy (Old Age, Sickness, Death):** Dhammarato advises the student to ask: **"What will steal my joy?"** The answer is often the fear of old age, sickness, and death. He reframes these not as tragedies, but as the **"lucky" way** to live (getting sick before you die, getting old before you die). Contemplating death can help one appreciate the present moment and become "un-robbable" by the devil (or by fear).

5.  **Counter-Instinctual Nature of Dhamma:** The entire teaching is **counter-instinctual**. It asks us to drop baggage rather than gain things, to apologize with joy rather than shame, and to stop blaming others. It is a "negative sell"—you lose your defilements to uncover the enlightenment you already have.

6.  **The Teacher’s Journey:** Dhammarato shares his history of being "tricked" into teaching by Ajahn Buddhadasa, his time in the US, and his eventual move to teaching on the internet (Skype, videos) under the guidance of Ajahn Po and his wife, Tam. He notes that the internet format allows for **repeated, deep, individual guidance** that is more effective than occasional retreats.

7.  **Progress is Gradual:** The student notes that satisfaction and equanimity are growing, allowing him to **wait for defilements to drop away** naturally rather than trying to beat them out. Dhammarato confirms this is the right path—the Eightfold Noble Path—where right effort becomes easier and eventually "unremitting."

**Final Takeaway:** The path is not about achieving grand things, but about **skillfully letting go** of desire, fear, and blame. Through small, joyful intentions and a deep acceptance of mortality, one can find satisfaction here and now.

### Metaphors and Stories

This conversation between Speaker A and Speaker B is rich with metaphors and stories. Here is a breakdown of the key ones used:

### Metaphors

1.  **The Altitude and Marks:** Speaker A mentions Speaker B’s previous teaching about "altitude" and setting "marks" in a direction. This is a metaphor for navigating life’s path, where "altitude" represents a broad perspective or level of awareness, and "marks" are waypoints or intentions that guide one toward a general direction while staying present.

2.  **Deflating Desire (The Bladder/Balloon):** Speaker B describes Speaker A’s desire as "deflating," with the air "coming out of it." This metaphor compares desire to a balloon or bladder; as one progresses, the inflated, urgent need for things lessens, allowing for a calmer, more skillful state of being.

3.  **The Raft (Simile of the Raft):** Speaker B explicitly references the Buddha’s famous simile. The raft is a metaphor for the teachings (Dhamma) used to cross the "flood" of suffering. Once you reach the other shore (enlightenment), you don't carry the raft on your head—you let it go. Speaker B repurposes this, saying he now "teaches students how to build rafts."

4.  **The Huge Ship:** Speaker B uses the image of a "huge ship" that "does not turn around easily" due to momentum and drag. This is a metaphor for the human mind, which has the momentum of instinctual habits. It takes time, repeated effort, and wisdom to change its direction.

5.  **Taking the Glasses Off:** Speaker B says, "You need to take the glasses off and lose them so that you can see, in fact, that you are already enlightened." This is a metaphor for stripping away delusions and defilements. Our "glasses" (wrong views, cravings) distort reality, and enlightenment is not about *gaining* something new, but *losing* what obscures the innate truth.

6.  **The "Negative Sell":** This is a transactional metaphor for the Buddhist path. While other religions offer "a promise of something really good later" (a positive sell), Buddhism is a "negative sell" because it's about "losing rather than gaining"—losing defilements, baggage, and desires.

7.  **"Playing the Blame Game":** A colloquial metaphor for the instinctual habit of shifting responsibility to others, contrasted with the "counter-instinctual" practice of taking full responsibility for one's own mind.

### Stories

1.  **The Haiku (No Place to Go):** Speaker B tells a story through the lens of a classic Zen haiku: *“No place to go and nothing to do, and the spring comes and the grass grows by itself.”* This story/metaphor illustrates the ideal state of goallessness and effortless, natural progress on the path.

2.  **The Bangkok Smiling Trip:** Speaker B shares a personal story about a trip to Bangkok. Instead of succumbing to the restless hindrance of travel (being "out wandering"), he set a tiny, achievable goal: "to smile at everybody." He lists the taxi drivers, bus drivers, and stewardesses he smiled at. This story illustrates replacing grandiose, ego-driven goals with small, skillful intentions that create safety and practice Metta in daily life.

3.  **The Whistling Man in Hell (Cartoon):** Speaker B recounts a cartoon: A man is walking through a cave of horrors (bones, skeletons, devils) pushing a wheelbarrow of body parts, but he is "whistling" and saying "hiya" to the devils. The caption is the devils saying, "We're not getting through to this guy, are we?" This is a story about achieving a state of joy so profound that even the "devil" (death, fear, suffering) cannot rob you of your happiness.

4.  **Jimmy Durante’s Song ("Have You Ever Had the Feeling"):** Speaker B tells the story of the song by Jimmy Durante. The lyrics (“Do you want to go? Do you want to stay?”) and the physical comedy of the singer taking his hat off and putting it on are used to delve into the profound, Hamlet-like question of life and death: whether to "be or not to be." The key twist is that this deep contemplation is done "joyfully" rather than with trepidation.

5.  **The Origin Story of Teaching (Being "Tricked"):** Speaker B tells a personal story of how he became a teacher. He was "lovingly tricked" by his teacher, Bhikkhu Buddhadasa, who put him on the spot to translate and then finish a teaching in front of a large audience. This illustrates the traditional method of a teacher setting up a student to take on more responsibility.

6.  **The "Hands Off" Story (Ajahn Po and Tam):** Speaker B tells a recent story about his wife, Tam. When he planned a big retreat, she refused, saying he was "an old man." When he recognized she used a phrase identical to his old teacher Ajahn Po, it was revealed she had gone to Ajahn Po and demanded, “He's my husband now, he does not belong to you, you hands off.” This story illustrates the different "time frames" (eras) of his teaching life and the shift to online teaching.

7.  **The Goenka Retreat Reflection:** Speaker A shares a brief story of his own experience at a Goenka retreat. A talk about death "hit him hard" and made him sad for an hour. This story serves to confirm Speaker B’s point that contemplating death, while difficult, can lead to a deeper appreciation for the present moment.
