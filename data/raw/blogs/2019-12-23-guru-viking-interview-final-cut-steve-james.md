- Dhammarato began with Hindu gurus (Muktananda, Sai Baba, Osho) and Goenka’s Vipassana, initially seeking magical experiences.
- He became dissatisfied with the "magic" and "Hindu hoochie-goochie" of these systems.
- He met a Thai monk who told him to discard the *Visuddhimagga* and go see **Buddhadasa Bhikkhu** in Thailand. This encounter shifted him from "Buddha magical" to "Buddha reality."

**2. Buddhadasa & the Supramundane Dhamma**
- Buddhadasa is presented as a revolutionary who removed the "clog" in Buddhism by teaching the **Supramundane Dhamma** (the real, liberating teachings) openly.
- This teaching focuses on **Anatta** (no-self) and the practical removal of suffering (dissatisfaction) here and now, rather than relying on rituals or beliefs about future lives.
- Dhammarato traces his lineage back through Buddhadasa to the Thai royal family, emphasizing that this authentic teaching has always existed as a "hidden" thread.

**3. Critique of the *Visuddhimagga* (Buddhaghosa)**
- Dhammarato is highly critical of the *Visuddhimagga*, calling its author either a **"charlatan or a fool"** and a "bookworm with no experience."
- **Key Criticisms:**
    - It **intentionally removed** key elements of correct practice (like the "Aha, I caught you" moment of gladdening the mind).
    - It presents **Paticcasamuppada** (Dependent Origination) across **3 lifetimes** (past, future, rebirth), making it impossible to practice. Dhammarato says it should be understood as **past moment, present moment, and future moment**.
    - It promotes a **magical belief in Kamma** (doing good now for a reward in a future life), which prevents people from practicing correctly in the present.

**4. Correct Practice (The "Real" Dhamma)**
- **Goal:** To be free from the **five hindrances** (boredom, doubt, desire, aversion, dullness) *right now*.
- **Key Technique (Anapanasati):**
    - **Sati (Mindfulness):** Remembering to look at what the mind is doing.
    - **The "Aha" Moment:** When distracted, the correct practice is not just to "come back" to the breath, but to **gladden the mind** by saying "Aha, I caught you!" This turns you from a victim into a champion.
    - **Right Effort:** Actively pulling yourself out of "junk thought" into the present moment.
    - **Breathing:** Using deeper breathing to oxygenate the mind and keep it from dullness.
- **Attitude:** The correct attitude is that of a **lion/winnner**, not a victim. Practice should be **natural** and spontaneous, not a rigid, grade-school method.

**5. Critique of Western & Pragmatic Buddhism**
- **Western Buddhism:** Often fails because it is a "religion" filled with magical beliefs in rebirth and distant enlightenment. People practice with a "future results" mindset (I will practice hard to get something later), which is wrong view.
- **The Four Path Model (Stream-Enterer to Arahant):** He rejects the idea of **permanent attainments**. Like shaving a beard, you must continuously maintain the practice (Anicca - impermanence).
    - *Stream Entry* is not a magical event; it is knowing that you **can** clean the mind of hindrances.
    - *Arahantship* is not a title. It is a **reflection** over a long time that you are free from anger, fear of death, and competitiveness. You don't *become* an Arahant; you realize you are no longer suffering.

**6. Teaching Style & Closing**
- **Private Teaching:** Dhammarato teaches via Skype (often unrecorded) because the Dhamma is best taught one-on-one as "spiritual friends," not in a hierarchy of "teacher/student."
- **The "Label" Trap:** He dismisses the value of claiming enlightenment or being an Arahant, as these labels create dissatisfaction (wanting more) and ego (one-up/one-down). The real goal is to be **completely satisfied right now**.
- **Final Advice:** "Don't worry, be happy."

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the talk, here are the key metaphors and stories used by Speaker C (Dhammarato):

**Metaphors:**

1.  **The Clog in the Drain:** Used to describe Buddhadasa's role in Buddhism. He "found the clog in the drain and removed the clog," which "upset a whole lot of frogs and swamp people." This metaphor illustrates how his correct teaching disturbed the comfortable, stagnant, or corrupt elements of the religious establishment.

2.  **The Pony in the Horseshit:** Referring to the "Pragmatic Dharma" movement. Dhammarato says they realized Western Buddhism "flat out don't work," but they know "there's got to be a pony in that pile of horseshit somewhere." This implies that while the mainstream tradition is mostly useless (the shit), there is a valuable, real truth (the pony) hidden within it.

3.  **Shaving a Beard:** Used to explain the nature of attainment and impermanence (*anicca*). He says that achieving a clean state (like stream entry) is like shaving a long beard—it's a lot of work. But you don't stay shaved forever; the beard grows back. The skill, however, becomes exquisite, and eventually, you only have to deal with the occasional "weed."

4.  **The Gardener / Weeds in the Garden:** A related metaphor to the shaving. "There will always be weeds in the garden, but after 5 or 6 years, not so many, only an occasional weed." This illustrates that while enlightenment is a process of constant maintenance, the frequency and power of the "weeds" (hindrances, defilements) diminish over time with correct practice.

5.  **Grade School / First Grade Retreat:** A critique of the Goenka retreat model. He compares it to a school where everyone, regardless of progress, does the same 10-day first-grade retreat over and over. Just as a fourth-grader doesn't need to keep repeating first grade, advanced practitioners need individualized, natural instruction, not a rigid, one-size-fits-all method.

6.  **One-Up, One-Down System:** A metaphor for the unhealthy power dynamic in many teacher-student relationships (and psychotherapist-client relationships). He contrasts this with the ideal of spiritual friendship (*kalyāṇa-mittatā*), where teacher and student become "friends... equals... up here together."

7.  **The Kamma Machine:** A humorous, critical metaphor for a popular misunderstanding of karma. People think if they die with bad karma, "the Kamma machine is going to dig me up just so he can slap me around." This paints the literal belief in future-life karmic retribution as a mechanical, punishing, and absurd system.

**Stories:**

1.  **The Story of Buddhadasa's "Trial" (Sangha Sesa):** This is a central narrative. Dhammarato tells how Buddhadasa's public talk on the "Supramundane Dhamma" upset traditional monks, leading to a formal ecclesiastical trial. The story highlights the dangerous, revolutionary nature of the true teachings and how Buddhadasa's validation came from a high-ranking monk (his future teacher), not from the institutional majority.

2.  **The Story of Ajahn Po Waiting at the Gate:** This is Dhammarato's personal "magical" experience. When he first arrived at Wat Suan Mokkh on a bicycle, the abbot, Ajahn Po, was standing at the gate and said, "Where have you been? I have been waiting for you." Dhammarato initially saw this as mystical, but he then deconstructs it rationally: Ajahn Po had seen him bicycling down the road earlier and simply deduced he was coming to the monastery. This story serves to reinforce his point that the Dhamma is based on "real reality," not magic.

3.  **The Story of the Indian Woman and the Newspaper:** To illustrate the charlatan nature of Sai Baba, Dhammarato tells of meeting a woman on a train in India who showed him a newspaper with "page after page after page" of evidence proving Sai Baba's "magic" was sleight of hand. Despite this exposure, he notes that "still, when I went to see him, it was a huge crowd," demonstrating the power of belief over evidence.

4.  **The Story of the King of Thailand at Wat Suan Mokkh:** He tells of "the first time that I ever saw the King of Thailand" at a dedication ceremony for Buddhadasa. This story serves to legitimize his lineage, tracing it back to the royal family and the monk-prince who disrobed. It shows that the "Supramundane Dhamma" had elite, hidden support within the Thai establishment.

5.  **The Story of the "Right Thing to the Wrong People":** This is the outcome of Buddhadasa's Sangha trial. The council's official ruling was that Buddhadasa was "teaching the right thing to the wrong people." This story functions as a key principle: the real Dhamma is not for everyone; it is for the few who are ready, and it must be taught privately and carefully.
