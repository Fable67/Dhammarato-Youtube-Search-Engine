### Summary

Here is a summary of the Dhamma talk between Dhammarato and a Sangha friend (Speaker B).

### Core Theme: The Shift from "Ordinary Right View" to "Noble Right View" (Right Attitude)

The talk focuses on redefining what "Right Attitude" (Samma Diṭṭhi) really means in practice. The key is moving from a mindset of **expectation and judgment** to one of **investigation and satisfaction.**

---

### Key Points from the Discussion:

**1. The Problem with Expectations (Speaker B's Experience)**
- Speaker B has been practicing for over two years and notices that when he drops expectations about how he *should* be performing, his practice becomes more peaceful and enjoyable.
- However, he still gets lost in thought frequently and feels frustrated by this lack of progress.

**2. The Main Attitude Change (Dhammarato's Response)**
- **Do not mourn the wandering mind; be glad you caught it.** This is the fundamental shift in attitude.
- Being satisfied with "the way things are" (including your current level of distraction) is more productive than being disappointed that you are not further along.

**3. Ordinary vs. Noble (Supramundane) Right View**
- **Ordinary Right View:** Operates on the "law of Kamma" (if I do good, I get good results). This is a worldly, cause-and-effect mindset that naturally leads to suffering when expectations aren't met (e.g., "I practiced, so I should be calm").
- **Noble (Supramundane) Right View:** Takes the law of Kamma into account but **removes the expectation of future results.** It focuses on seeing things clearly *now*. This leads to freedom from suffering.

**4. The Analogy of the Marine**
- A raw recruit may *think* they are a Marine because they joined, but the Drill Instructor knows they are not yet a Marine. They must go through the mud, the barbed wire, and the tests.
- Similarly, a practitioner can claim "Right View" intellectually, but they must go through the difficult work of investigation and confession to embody it. The dedication comes from **Right Intention**, but the *attitude* is the steady, grounded stance of one who *has* already done the work.

**5. The "Victim Language" and Growing Up**
- Words like "try," "hard," "difficult," "should," and "ought" are the language of a victim.
- Humans are born as infants (dependent, victims of circumstances). Growing up means claiming your own territory and taking responsibility, rather than transferring authority from mommy to the police to the government.
- **Noble Right View** sees authorities but does not *cling* to them as "I, me, and mine." You can laugh at the "trip" they are running.

**6. Investigation and Confession (The Practical Path)**
- **How to Investigate:** Start with the Four Foundations of Mindfulness (body, feelings, mind, mental objects).
    - **Gladden the mind:** When you see a hindrance, don't reject it; gladden the mind with an "Aha, I caught you!" This turns **doubt into curiosity**.
    - Curiosity is the true tool for investigation.
- **The Role of Confession (Admitting Wrongdoing):**
    - To eliminate suffering, one must admit their own bad behavior.
    - **The Noble Attitude:** You can confess with joy because you are *freeing* yourself from the burden of that wrongdoing, rather than feeling humiliated and defensive.
    - This mirrors the monastic practice of the Patimokkha (confession ceremony), where the Sangha is purified and re-established every full moon.

**7. The Three Types of Feeling & Ignorance**
- The root of suffering (Second Noble Truth) lies in **my greed, my ill will, and my delusion.**
- The "third feeling" (neither liked nor disliked) is often mistaken for neutral. Dhammarato clarifies it is actually **confusion** ("I don't know if I like it or not"). This confusion is a powerful motivator (e.g., starting a war out of uncertainty).
- Real progress is made when you recognize these subtle, confused feelings that you previously couldn't see, even if you *feel* like you are making no progress.

### Final Takeaway for the Practitioner

**Satisfaction is the fuel for progress.** Judgment and craving for results are the obstacles. The goal is to cultivate a "gyroscopic" attitude—so stable and vertical that external storms (like a bad day or a wandering mind) don't knock you over. Instead of trying to *become* a lion, you practice having the *attitude* of a lion right now.

### Metaphors and Stories

Here is a breakdown of the key metaphors and stories used in the talk, organized by the type of illustration:

### I. Core Metaphors for the Right Attitude

These metaphors are used to explain the difference between a "victim" mindset (ordinary right view) and a "noble" or "winner" mindset (supramundane right view).

- **The Lion / Rhinoceros:**
    - **Metaphor:** The speaker contrasts the attitude of someone who merely *intends* to be a lion with the attitude of the lion itself.
    - **Meaning:** You cannot *try* to have the attitude of a winner; you must *be* the winner. It's about identity, not aspiration. The "victim" says "I want to be a rhino," while the "noble" person acts with the innate confidence and power of the rhino.

- **The Marine Boot Camp:**
    - **Story:** A raw recruit joins the Marines and immediately sees himself as a Marine. The Drill Instructor (DI) does not see him that way. The recruit must endure the grueling test of boot camp (crawling under barbed wire, etc.) before he truly *knows* he is a Marine.
    - **Meaning:** This illustrates the difference between a conceptual belief (joined the Marines = am a Marine) and direct, experiential knowledge (survived boot camp = know I am a Marine). The "satisfaction" comes from the knowledge gained through the process, not from the title.

- **The Skyscraper (Engineering Attitude):**
    - **Metaphor:** A tall building is only strong if it is perfectly vertical. If it leans even slightly, it is vulnerable to wind (life's annoyances, like a missed paycheck) and earthquakes (major upheavals, like a relationship ending).
    - **Meaning:** The "right attitude" is a state of perfect balance and alignment (vertical). When you are balanced, external forces do not knock you down. You are stable because your "center of gravity" is correct.

- **The Airplane Gyroscope (Attitude vs. Altitude):**
    - **Metaphor:** The gyroscopic autopilot's primary job is to keep the plane at the *right attitude* (its orientation in space). If the attitude is wrong, the plane will lose altitude (fall) regardless of its power. The speaker also tells the story of IBM using gyroscopes on WWII battleships to calculate when to fire the guns as the ship rocked, allowing them to hit a target like Dresden.
    - **Meaning:** The "attitude" of the mind is more fundamental than "altitude" (worldly success or progress). If your mental attitude (view) is correct, everything else falls into place. Just as a ship's stability determines the accuracy of its fire, your inner stability determines the effectiveness of your actions.

### II. Metaphors for the Problematic Default State (The Victim)

These illustrate the "ordinary right view" that leads to suffering.

- **The Victim / Top Dog vs. Underdog (Fritz Perls & Eric Byrne):**
    - **Metaphor:** The speaker references psychological models where one part of the self (the "Top Dog") judges and controls another (the "Underdog").
    - **Meaning:** This is the mentality of "I'm not okay, you're okay" or the "church lady" looking down on others. The speaker argues the Western psychotherapy system is flawed because it reinforces this dynamic (the therapist is the Top Dog, the client is the Underdog).

- **The Church Lady / The Police Officer:**
    - **Metaphor:** The speaker uses the examples of a "church lady" who judges non-attendees and a police officer who enforces the law.
    - **Meaning:** These are figures who enforce the "law of Kamma" (behavior has consequences) from a place of authority and judgment. They represent the *ordinary* right view, where one is still focused on external rules, punishment, and reward (avoiding hell, getting into heaven). This is a state of dependency.

- **The Unfinished Child / The Authority Hopper:**
    - **Metaphor:** The speaker describes a developmental process where people move from one authority figure to the next (Mommy, Daddy, Teacher, Cop, Priest, Boss, Government, Social Security) without ever "growing up."
    - **Meaning:** This is the default human state of being a "victim" or dependent. One never claims their own "territory" and remains subject to external authorities. Nobility means seeing the authorities but not clinging to them as "I, me, and mine."

### III. Stories and Examples for the Path (Investigation & Confession)

These illustrate the active practice of the "noble" attitude.

- **The "Aha, I Caught You!" (Gladdening the Mind):**
    - **Metaphor:** Instead of being disappointed that the mind wandered (like a victim), the speaker suggests the attitude of "Aha, I've caught you!" (like a detective or a game).
    - **Meaning:** This is the key "attitude change." It transforms doubt (a hindrance) into curiosity (an investigative tool). You are happy to see the mind's wandering, not sad that you got lost. This is the "gladdening" that fuels investigation.

- **The Catholic Confessional / The Monk's Confession (Patimokkha):**
    - **Story:** The speaker contrasts the corrupted Catholic confessional (a profit machine that keeps people sinning) with the Buddhist practice of the Patimokkha (where monks confess to be truly *free* from their wrongdoings and re-establish the purity of the Sangha).
    - **Meaning:** True rehabilitation requires owning one's bad behavior, confessing it, and doing the "retribution" (work) to be free. The goal is not to be punished but to be liberated. The story of the *Godfather* giving $100 million to the Church illustrates the corrupt version: the institution has no incentive to *stop* the wrongdoing because it profits from it.

- **The Thai Word "Kotut" (Please Beat My Ass):**
    - **Story:** The speaker translates the Thai word for "excuse me" as "Beat my ass" / "Please beat my butt."
    - **Meaning:** This illustrates the universal cultural need for apology and rehabilitation. The "noble" attitude is to willingly offer oneself up for this "beating" (humiliation, confession, making amends) with joy because the intention is to be free from suffering, not to avoid punishment. It's a proactive, liberating act, not a passive, painful one.

- **The "Third Kind of Feeling" (Curiosity as a Motivator):**
    - **Story:** The speaker gives the example of seeing a stranger from a distance (a revenuer, a Viet Cong, a pretty girl) and the resulting feeling of curiosity/confusion.
    - **Meaning:** This feeling of "not knowing if I like it or not" is a powerful motivator. It can lead to war (attack out of uncertainty) or to investigation. The "supramundane" path uses this energy to *investigate* reality rather than react out of ignorance.
