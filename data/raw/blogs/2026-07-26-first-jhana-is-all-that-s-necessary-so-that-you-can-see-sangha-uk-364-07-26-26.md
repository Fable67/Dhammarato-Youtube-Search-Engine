### Summary of the Dhamma Talk

The talk covers three main themes: a correction on the nature of Jhana, a detailed explanation of the four kinds of Kamma, and a critique of modern retreat practice versus traditional seclusion.

**1. On Jhana (Meditative Absorption)**

- **The Core Point:** The Buddha’s path to enlightenment only requires the **First Jhana**. This state provides enough mental stability and clarity to see clearly and understand all aspects of Dependent Origination (Paticcasamuppada), free from the hindrances.
- **Correction on "8 Jhanas":** The idea of eight jhanas is a later addition (from the 5th-century *Visuddhimagga*), not from the Buddha's original teachings.
    - The four "formless attainments" (arupa samapatti) were never called jhanas in the original suttas; they were just listed after the four jhanas, leading to confusion.
    - The "anti-Asoke" movement in Thailand (meaning "before Asoka") aimed to abandon later commentaries and return to the original suttas.
- **First Jhana as Enlightenment:** Dhammarato suggests that if the First Jhana is the quenching of the hindrances and brings *sukkha* (satisfaction), then it can be seen as the end of *dukkha* (dissatisfaction) in that moment. Thus, **the path to enlightenment (First Jhana) is essentially enlightenment itself when experienced.**
- **Magical Thinking:** The idea that jhanas grant magical powers or are only for monks is dismissed as incorrect Hindu-influenced thinking.

**2. On Kamma (Action)**

- **The Problem with Simple Kamma:** The common religious idea that "good actions bring good results, bad actions bring bad results" is flawed. A 3-year-old knows you can sometimes get away with bad actions. In fact, the results of actions are one of the "Four Imponderables"—we simply cannot know them.
- **The Four Kinds of Kamma (from MN 57 - The Dog-Duty Ascetic):**
    1.  **Dark with Dark Result:** Bad actions that lead to bad outcomes (e.g., the dog-duty ascetic who fails to become a dog will end up in a "hell" of disappointment).
    2.  **Bright with Bright Result:** Good actions that lead to good outcomes.
    3.  **Mixed (Dark & Bright) with Mixed Result:** This is the most common type of action for ordinary people. Most actions have mixed motivations (e.g., a doctor who wants to help but also wants profit; a musician who wants art but also fame). The results will be a mixture of good and bad.
    4.  **Neither Dark nor Bright, Leading to the End of Action:** This is the Dhamma. This is an action where the benefit is gained immediately. There is no future payoff to crave.
- **The Goal:** The aim is to practice in a way that you don't accumulate fresh kamma. This is done through seclusion and non-doing. If you are alone, you are not engaging in mixed actions with others.

**3. On Retreats vs. Seclusion**

- **Origin of Retreats:** The modern 10-day retreat was invented in Burma in the 1950s as a morality program for government accountants, later popularized by Goenka. Dhammarato notes this is a recent invention, not the Buddha's practice.
- **The Problem with Retreats:** A retreat with 100 people is the opposite of seclusion. "Noble silence" is just silence of the mouth, but the mind is still interacting with others. The experience is a "mixed bag" of good and bad.
- **The Solution:** Instead of an expensive retreat center, buy camping gear and **go into the woods alone.** This is true seclusion.
- **Example: Wat Suan Mokkh:** The retreats there (which Dhammarato helped set up) are closer to a monk's lifestyle than Goenka's, with shorter sits, no "strong determination" to endure pain, and a focus on living a secluded life afterward, not just a 10-day course. The real practice is to stay in a kuti and "do nothing," cleaning the mind through Anapanasati (mindfulness of breathing).

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the talk, here are the metaphors and stories used by the speaker (Dhammarato) to illustrate his points about the Dhamma, Jhanas, Kamma, and practice:

### Metaphors

1.  **Writing in Sand, Stone, and Air (regarding Kamma/Intention):**
    - **The Hindu/Brahmin view:** "Speaking something was like writing it in the sand, and doing something was like chiseling it in stone, but just thinking about it was like writing it in the air."
    - **The Buddha’s turn around:** The speaker uses this metaphor to show that the Buddha rejected this hierarchy of action. Instead, the Buddha said, "the mind is the forerunner." The metaphor highlights that thought (mental action) is paramount, not the physical or verbal act.

2.  **"Mixed Bag" (regarding the nature of most Kamma):**
    - The speaker uses this metaphor repeatedly to describe how most actions are not purely good or bad, but are "mixed with both good and bad" in their motivations and results. This serves to deconstruct the simplistic religious idea that good deeds always lead to good outcomes.

3.  **The "Wank" (regarding Hindu practices like Jhanas for merging with Brahman):**
    - The speaker uses this crude, informal metaphor ("a bunch of wank") to dismiss the idea of merging a self with a higher self or achieving "oneness," stating this is a Hindu concept, not a Buddhist one.

4.  **Muay Thai Boxing Club vs. Monk’s Lifestyle (regarding retreats):**
    - He contrasts the Goenka-style retreat (which he calls like a "Muay Thai boxing club," implying discipline, pain, and endurance) with the Wat Suan Mokkh retreat (which he says is closer to the "lifestyle of a monk," implying gentleness and seclusion).

### Stories (Anecdotes and Sutta Narratives)

1.  **The Dog-Duty Ascetic and Cow-Duty Ascetic (Sutta MN 57):**
    - **The Story:** Two ascetics—one pretending to be a dog (crawling, barking, eating off the ground) and one pretending to be a cow—come to the Buddha to ask about their practice.
    - **The Point:** The Buddha tells them that if they are *very* good at pretending, they will become a dog or a cow. But if they fail (which is likely), they will go to hell (interpreted by the speaker as "the disappointment of trying to be something they are not").
    - **Application:** The speaker uses this to illustrate that "good" actions don't always lead to "good" results. These ascetics thought they were doing something holy, but the result was either animal rebirth or hell.

2.  **The Emperor and the Doctor (China):**
    - **The Story:** An emperor in China offered to pay a doctor a regular fee to keep him *healthy*. If the emperor got sick, the doctor would *not* get paid.
    - **The Point:** This story illustrates the "mixed bag" of Kamma in the medical profession. The speaker argues that doctors (and pharmaceutical companies) are often in the "business of keeping people sick" to make money, rather than actually curing them.

3.  **Jesus and the Praying Jews (Bible):**
    - **The Story:** Jesus said that Jews who prayed in public in fine clothes got their benefit *right then* (showing off), but those who prayed in private got a better benefit (cleaning their own heart).
    - **The Point:** This is used to explain the "fourth kind of Kamma" (neither bright nor dark, leading to the end of action). The benefit is immediate, not deferred to the future.

4.  **Tudong / The Walkabout:**
    - **The Story:** The speaker describes *tudong* (traditionally an austerity of wandering) as a "walkabout" where monks go camping or hiking through the wilderness.
    - **The Point:** This is an example of an action that leads to seclusion, reducing "doing" and allowing for the practice of cleaning the mind.

5.  **The Origin of the 10-Day Retreat (Burma, 1950s):**
    - **The Story:** The speaker explains that the modern 10-day retreat was not from the Buddha but was invented in the 1950s in Burma by Ledi Sayadaw for a group of accountants in the new Burmese government's Treasury Department.
    - **The Point:** The speaker uses this historical story to argue that retreats were a modern invention for a specific purpose (teaching morality to accountants), and are not the same as the Buddha's teaching of true seclusion.

6.  **Bhikkhu Buddhadasa and the CIA (Political Context):**
    - **The Story:** Bhikkhu Buddhadasa was used as a "mascot" by armed rebel groups during the Vietnam War in Thailand, which caused him to get in trouble with the CIA and the Thai government.
    - **The Point:** This story is used to illustrate the "Anti-Asoke" movement (going back to the original Suttas) and how political forces can co-opt religious figures and teachings.

7.  **Achan Po’s Retreat (Personal Anecdote):**
    - **The Story:** When the speaker (Dhammarato) signed up for a retreat at Wat Suan Mokkh, the abbot (Achan Po) scolded him: "Why are you in this retreat? Go get a kuti over at Wat Sumen Monk and hang out and do nothing like the way that the monks do."
    - **The Point:** This personal story reinforces the idea that a formal, crowded retreat is inferior to genuine, private seclusion.

### Key Concepts Framed by these Stories/Metaphors:

- **Jhanas:** The speaker uses the "8 Jhanas" vs. "4 Jhanas" debate as a story about the corruption of the original teaching by later commentaries (Visuddhimagga).
- **Kamma:** The primary lesson is that "we do not know the results of our actions." The dog/cow ascetic story is the core illustration of this.
- **Practice:** The speaker uses the story of the "Tudong" and the "Achan Po" anecdote to argue for going into the wilderness alone, rather than going to expensive, crowded retreats.
