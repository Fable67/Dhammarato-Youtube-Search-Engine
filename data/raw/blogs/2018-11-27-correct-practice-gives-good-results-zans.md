### Summary

Here is a summary of the Dhamma talk between Dhammarato (Speaker A) and Sangha friend (Speaker B):

**Core Issue:**
Speaker B is experiencing overwhelming, intense "energy" in his body (especially the head) during meditation, accompanied by fear, a surreal/dreamlike state, and heat. He feels victimized by this phenomenon, which he links to experiences on retreat and possible "Kundalini."

**Dhammarato's Diagnosis:**
Dhammarato identifies that Speaker B is **practicing incorrectly**. He argues that the Buddha's path leads to *cooling down* (Nibbana) and relaxation, not to heat, agitation, or terror. He tells Speaker B that he is "manufacturing" this energy through wrong effort and wrong thinking, even if unintentionally.

**Key Instructions (The Remedy):**
1.  **Start Over (Back to Step 1):** Forget all previous experiences and concepts. The practice is always new, beginning from this present moment.
2.  **Right Mindfulness (Sati):** Remember to stop thinking about the past (including the "energy") and the future. Throw out the hindrances.
3.  **Right View:** The correct perspective is: "It doesn't matter what I was thinking before. I am going to enjoy *this* moment."
4.  **Right Effort:** It takes active effort to drop the painful thoughts and choose to be happy right now.
5.  **Right Attitude:** Stop playing the victim. Change the mentality to: "I can do this. I am successful. I can be happy right now."

**The Bottom Line:**
The practice is not about achieving special states or managing "energy." It is about **cleaning the mind, taking a deep happy breath, and enjoying the freedom of the present moment—over and over again.** Dhammarato asks Speaker B to call back in 1-2 days to continue working on this foundational re-set.

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the talk, here are the key metaphors and stories used, primarily by **Speaker A** (the teacher):

### 1. The **Ladder** Metaphor
- **Usage:** Speaker A uses this to explain the necessity of returning to the basics when practice goes wrong.
- **Quote:** *"What happens if you're climbing a ladder and you're up to like the 9th or the 10th rung and you fall off the ladder? How do you get back on the ladder again to step 9? Do you just jump back up to step 9, or do you have to start again?"*
- **Meaning:** You cannot skip steps. If you fall (get lost in wrong practice), you must go back to the first rung (step 1) rather than trying to resume from where you were.

### 2. The **Cooling Pie / Nibbana as Cooling** Metaphor
- **Usage:** Speaker A redefines the Buddhist concept of *Nibbana* (often translated as "extinguishing") using this metaphor to counter Speaker B’s obsession with "heat" and "energy."
- **Quote:** *"It's a whole lot more of doing with cooling off, in the sense of taking a pie out of the oven, a pizza pie or an apple pie or something, out of the oven and letting it cool. Because if you eat it when it comes right out of the oven, it'll burn your mouth. All right, so you let the food cool. That's Nibbana."*
- **Meaning:** The goal is not to violently extinguish (like throwing water on a fire), but to allow the mind/body to settle and cool naturally. "Energy" is heat; Nibbana is coolness.

### 3. The **Trained Horse (Nibbana)** Metaphor
- **Usage:** Speaker A offers a second interpretation of the word *Nibbana* to emphasize the idea of training and taming, rather than destruction.
- **Quote:** *"The word is used when you can train an animal. For instance, you can get a horse, and if the horse is a wild horse, then in the Pali it would be, the training process would be Nibbana, so that the horse would be Nibbana when it was trained, so that it could be put to work."*
- **Meaning:** The mind is like a wild horse (full of untamed energy). Nibbana is the process of training it so it becomes useful and calm, not just "burning off" the energy.

### 4. The **"Never Mind, Start Again"** Story / Practice
- **Usage:** This is a direct instruction and a recurring theme, not a fictional story, but a methodology for always returning to the present.
- **Quote:** *"Never mind, start again. The problem is right now, as everybody— everybody needs to never mind, start again. We always start back over at step 1."*
- **Meaning:** This is the core "story" of the practice: no matter how far off you go, you simply drop the past and restart in the present moment. It is a direct remedy to the story of "victimhood" that Speaker A accuses Speaker B of playing.

### 5. The **"Playing the Game of Victim"** Narrative
- **Usage:** Speaker A frames Speaker B’s experience as a psychological narrative (a "game") rather than an objective reality.
- **Quote:** *"And right now I can tell that, no, you're playing the game of victim. 'I am being victimized by this energy, and I don't know where it came from. It just came up by itself.'"*
- **Meaning:** Speaker B is telling a story about himself where he is powerless. Speaker A challenges this, suggesting he is actually *creating* the energy, and thus can choose to stop.

### Summary of the Core Conflict:
- **Speaker B’s Metaphor (Implicit):** The experience is like a **volcano** or **possession**—an overwhelming, external force (energy, heat) that he is a victim of.
- **Speaker A’s Metaphor:** The experience is like a **burning pie** or a **wild horse**—something that must be cooled or trained through consistent, gentle effort (the ladder). The student is not a victim, but a **cook** or **trainer** who just fell off the ladder and needs to start climbing again from the bottom.
