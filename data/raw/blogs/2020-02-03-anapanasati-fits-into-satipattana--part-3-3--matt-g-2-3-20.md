### Summary

Here is a summary of the Dhamma talk between Dhammarato and a student named Matt (Speaker B):

**Core Theme**
The talk explores the unity of mystical experiences across different spiritual traditions (Theravada Buddhism and Kashmir Shaivism/Tantra), while emphasizing the practical, non-magical, and "negative" (letting go) approach of the Buddha's teachings. The discussion highlights that while different paths use different metaphors (e.g., "Self" vs. "Emptiness"), they point to the same ineffable, expansive state of being.

**Key Points of Discussion**

1.  **Bridging Traditions (Tantra vs. Buddhism)**
    - Matt describes a practice from his yoga philosophy teacher involving "Spanda" (expansion/contraction) and contacting a "pure eye awareness" or a boundless Self.
    - Dhammarato identifies this as a metaphor for **Samsara** (the cycle of becoming) and **Anicca** (impermanence/constant flux), noting the Buddha also spoke of "expansion and contraction."
    - The conversation concludes that both traditions are pointing to the same thing: getting out of the "little self" (the source of Dukkha) into an expansive, unified experience. The differences are merely in the metaphors and terminology (e.g., "boundless Self" vs. "Emptiness").

2.  **The Practice of "Letting the Scene Be Merely the Scene"**
    - Dhammarato explains a deep meditative state described by Matt as "black luminosity" or "luminous emptiness."
    - He frames this as stopping the conceptual processing of experience. Instead of clinging, craving, or analyzing (moving down the chain of *Paticcasamuppada*), the practitioner stays at the level of pure sensory input ("data buffering") without letting the mind "fix" on or own the experience.
    - This leads to a state of awe, gratitude, and satisfaction, described as a **"pleasant abiding"** related to the Fourth Jhana, where boundaries (of body/self) dissolve.

3.  **The Danger of Magical Thinking & The Four Imponderables**
    - Dhammarato warns against turning these experiences into "magical thinking" (e.g., believing the universe is "made of consciousness" or that there is a permanent eternal Self).
    - He argues that all systems (including science and religion) are just refined metaphors. The Buddha's approach is to recognize what is **unknowable** (the Four Imponderables: origins of the cosmos, extent of kamma, the nature of the Tathagata after death, etc.).
    - **Wisdom** is recognizing "you don't know nothing" and resting in the present moment rather than trying to solve cosmic puzzles.

4.  **The "Negative" Path (Via Negativa) as the Cure**
    - Dhammarato contrasts the Tantric emphasis on "fullness" with the Buddha's emphasis on **Dukkha and Nirodha** (suffering and its cessation).
    - The path is about **diagnosing and eliminating the disease**: stopping the behaviors and attachments that cause pain.
    - This includes simplifying life (fewer possessions, lower expectations) and dropping the "little self" by surrendering control (e.g., Dhammarato's advice to Matt to call his estranged partner "Boss Lady" to let go of conflict and opinion).

5.  **The Seven Factors of Enlightenment**
    - Dhammarato explains that these factors (Mindfulness, Investigation, Energy, Rapture, Tranquility, Concentration, Equanimity) arise naturally when the "negative" work is done.
    - They are characterized as **"unremitting"** (spring-loaded), meaning the practitioner is effortlessly engaged, satisfied, and unified with the present moment without trying to gain anything.

**Final Analogy & Advice**
- The talk concludes with humor (the "make me one with everything" hot dog joke) and a practical takeaway: **the best way to live is to let go of the need to know, own, or control.** Practice is about getting back to the basics, dropping the victimhood of the "little self," and enjoying the "is-ness" of the present moment.
