### Summary

This is a summary of the Dhamma talk between **Dhammarato** and **Speaker B (a Sangha friend)** .

The core theme of the talk is the **psychological crisis of identity and maturity** (specifically in men) caused by the absence of clear "rites of passage" in modern society, and how this immaturity manifests in social behavior, politics, and even spiritual practice.

Here is a breakdown of the key points:

**1. The Problem: The Missing "Rite of Passage"**
- Modern society lacks a definitive event (like a Bar Mitzvah, a tribal survival quest, or Spartan trials) that marks the transition from child to adult.
- Because of this gap, young men experience an **identity crisis**. They are desperate to prove they are "men" but don't know how.
- **Speaker A** argues that behaviors like owning huge gun collections, big trucks, the "don't tread on me" attitude, and chest-pounding bravado are all **substitutes** for this missing rite of passage. These men are trying to convince *themselves* they are adults.
- Failed substitutes for true adulthood include: graduation (because there's always more school), marriage (a weak demarcation), and joining the military.

**2. The Deeper Issue: Authority and the "Draft Animal" Mentality**
- The talk links the lack of a rite of passage to how we are raised. Dhammarato compares the life of a child (following orders without rationale) to a **draft animal** (a horse plowing a field).
- The child is told "do your ABCs," "clean your room," etc., without being given a meaningful reason. This creates deep, unconscious **resentment**.
- Immature people (the "boys" who never became men) seek **authority over others** (e.g., running for office) to prove their manhood, rather than seeking **authority over their own minds**.
- The quote: *"The very best people that the U.S. has to offer to run for office wouldn't bother. It's only the worst people... the ones who want to prove their manhood."*

**3. The Dhamma Application: Resentment in Meditation**
- The talk then connects this psychological immaturity directly to meditation practice.
- Dhammarato points out that the deep-seated resentment from being raised like a "draft animal" (doing things because we *have* to, not because we *want* to) shows up in meditation.
- **The Pattern:** A meditator gets good at practice (clean house, homeostasis). Then "life is shit" (a difficult event occurs). The student reacts with resentment: *"I've been doing all this meditation. Why can't I count on it to get me out of this mess? I thought I had practiced enough."*
- This reveals a childish attitude: The meditator is treating the practice as a chore they "should" have finished, rather than a living, present-moment path. They want to be an "adult" who has arrived, but they are still acting like a child who doesn't want to do their homework.

**4. The Solution (Implied)**
- **Speaker B** offers a key insight: connect with such people (like the "gun guy") through **rationality**. If you treat them as capable of rational thought, you can open a window to talk about the mind.
- **Dhammarato** implies that the true "rite of passage" is learning to be the **authority over your own mind**—doing things out of wisdom and reason, not out of coercion or resentment. This is what makes a genuine adult (and a genuine meditator).

**In short:** The talk uses the social concept of "rites of passage" to diagnose a widespread psychological immaturity (especially in men). This immaturity, rooted in childhood obedience, creates resentment that poisons meditation practice, turning it into a chore rather than a path to genuine freedom and self-mastery.

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the provided transcript, the speaker (A) uses a central **metaphor** (the "draft animal") and several related **stories and analogies** (the "Rite of Passage" narrative) to explain the psychological development of men, societal immaturity, and the hidden resentment that hinders spiritual practice.

Here is a breakdown of the metaphors and stories used:

### 1. The Central Metaphor: The Draft Animal / The Child as an Animal

This is the most pervasive and powerful metaphor in the talk. Speaker A directly compares the experience of a child (and by extension, a resentful adult) to a draft animal (like a horse or ox).

- **The Comparison:** A draft animal does the work (plows the field) but does not receive the primary benefit (the harvest). It gets only enough (hay) to keep working. The authority figure (the farmer) gets the real reward.
- **The Application:** A child is told to do chores, homework, and sit still. They are given orders without sufficient reason or choice. They do the "work" of childhood but don't feel they receive the "harvest" (autonomy, respect, purpose).
- **The Consequence:** This creates deep-seated resentment. The child grows into an adult who does what he's told ("goes along to get along") but secretly resents all authority. This resentment is so ingrained it's invisible until it surfaces in meditation.

**Speaker A says:** *"...one of the qualities of the child is the same as the quality of a draft animal... we put our kids into school and say, you do your ABCs... by giving orders and telling children what to do kind of defines that as I'm the child or I'm the animal because I'm not in charge here."*

### 2. The Central Story/Analogy: The Missing "Rite of Passage"

This is the narrative framework for the entire first half of the talk. Speaker A uses the universal concept of a "rite of passage" to explain an identity crisis in young men.

- **The Problem:** Modern society lacks a clear, marked event that publicly and definitively transitions a boy into a man. Remnants exist (drinking age, voting age, bar mitzvah) but are not sufficient.
- **The Result (The "Don't Tread on Me" Guy):** Without this societal recognition, a young man feels he must **prove it to himself**. This leads to **"chest pounding and bravado"** — buying big trucks, collecting guns, aggressive posturing. Speaker A directly diagnoses this as an **"identity crisis"** born from a lack of a clear transition.
- **Failed Substitutes:** Speaker A lists modern substitutes that don't fully work:
    - **Graduation:** "Does not fulfill that bill" because "there's always more school."
    - **Marriage:** A partial substitute, as it marks commitment and duty, but it's often not a true internal transformation.

**Speaker A says:** *"And so these young men who were all into big trucks and gun collections and 'don't tread on me' is basically in an identity crisis. Because he doesn't know yet. He hasn't been able to prove to himself yet that he is now a man."*

### 3. Supporting Stories & Analogies (Examples of Rites of Passage)

To contrast the modern lack, Speaker A provides historical and cultural examples of functional rites of passage:

- **American Indian Tradition:** A young man goes into the wilderness alone for days, proving he can "forage for himself."
- **Modern Survival Retreats (Michigan):** "Give me a knife, and 2 weeks, and I'm on my own out there." This is a modern, intentional attempt to re-create the rite.
- **Spartan Tradition:** Young men had to go out with a spear for weeks and return with the skin of a dangerous animal (bear, lion) as proof of their ability.
- **The Analogy of the Aspiring Politician:** This is a direct application of the "proving manhood" narrative. Speaker A argues that the desire to be "an authority over others" is a sign of immaturity. The people who run for office are often those still trying to prove their manhood, making them "the worst people" for the job.

**Speaker A says:** *"...people who want to be the authority over others are the most immature people... the very best people that the U.S. has to offer to run for office wouldn't bother. It's only the worst people... that run for office, because that's kind of a rite of passage for them."*

### 4. The Final Metaphor: The Cow Pie & The Resentful Meditator

This is a powerful, everyday-life metaphor that brings the "draft animal" resentment into the context of meditation.

- **The Setup:** The speaker describes a meditator who has been practicing well, feeling good ("homeostasis").
- **The Event:** "Life is shit. Some shit's gonna happen. This dude... steps into a cow pie."
- **The Reaction:** Instead of responding mindfully, the meditator's hidden resentment surfaces: *"Look, I've been doing all of this meditation. Why can't I count on the meditation to have gotten me out of this mess that I've made?"*
- **The Deeper Application:** This reaction reveals that the meditator was treating meditation like a chore (like a draft animal). He was doing it because he "should," and now he resents that it didn't protect him. This is the ultimate sign that the "child" mentality of doing things without intrinsic purpose is still present.

**Speaker A says:** *"Do you hear those thoughts that I'm revealing? Ah, so actually, even for the advanced meditator, we will make meditation as part of that which is done because it's supposed to be done. It's supposed to be good for us."*
