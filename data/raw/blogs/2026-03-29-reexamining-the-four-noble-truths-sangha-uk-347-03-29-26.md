### Summary

Here is a summary of the Dhamma talk given by **Dhammarato** with the Sangha friends on March 29th (likely 2020).

### Core Theme: The Second Noble Truth (The Cause of Dukkha)

The talk centers on redefining the Second Noble Truth (the cause of suffering) by comparing traditional Abhidhamma definitions with what is actually found in the early Suttas. Dhammarato argues that the common definition of "greed, ill will, and delusion" (*Lobha, Dosa, Moha*) is useful but not the original Sutta definition.

### Key Points and New Definitions

**1. The Sutta Definition of the Second Noble Truth (Tanha)**
- The actual cause of suffering, as found in Sutta 44 and the Dhammacakkha Sutta, is **Tanha** (Thirst/Craving) of three kinds:
    - **Kamma Tanha:** Craving for sense pleasures (sights, sounds, tastes, etc.). (Wanting the purse in the window).
    - **Bhava Tanha:** Craving to *become* something (wanting to be rich, enlightened, or a certain person).
    - **Vibhava Tanha:** Craving to *get rid of* something or *not be* something (wanting pain to stop, wanting to avoid the past, or suicidal ideation).

**2. The "Two Arrows" (Vedana vs. Tanha)**
- **First Arrow:** The raw physical sensation (e.g., pain in the knee).
- **Second Arrow:** The *mental* reaction of "I don't like this" (Vedana of dislike) which immediately turns into "I want this to stop" (Vibhava Tanha).
- **Key Insight:** You cannot always stop the first arrow (physical pain), but you can *always* stop the second arrow (the mental suffering of wanting it to be different).

**3. The Self and the Woeful States**
- **The Self is Born from Wanting:** When Tanha (wanting) appears, the "self" (the one who wants it) is created simultaneously.
- **The Four Woeful States (Heavy Dukkha):** These are not afterlife realms but *immediate mental states* caused by intense craving:
    1.  **Hell:** Being hot, bothered, and trapped (e.g., in a heated argument).
    2.  **Preta (Hungry Ghost):** Having a big belly (big desire) and a tiny mouth (inability to satisfy it).
    3.  **Animal:** Doing a job you don't like, being a "dumb animal" forced to work (the most common Western state).
    4.  **Asura:** Fear, trepidation, being all dressed up with nowhere to go (avoidance).

**4. The Practice: Seclusion and Mind States**
- **It’s Not the Object, It’s the Mind:** You cannot fix suffering by changing the world (e.g., getting a new job). You must change the *mind state* you bring to the world.
- **Seclusion (Kaya & Citta):** The essential practice is to physically get away (body seclusion) and then mentally get away from the "crap you brought with you" (mind seclusion). This allows you to see that the "not liking" is an internal habit, not a reaction to external things.
- **"Aha, I see you":** The goal is to see Dukkha with clarity and pleasure, rather than immediately trying to fix it or run from it.

**5. Practical Advice (Q&A)**
- **On Insomnia (Tom):** Drop the rule that says "I must sleep." Instead, enjoy lying in bed and being happy. The war is created by the craving to sleep, not by the wakefulness.
- **On "I don't like my job" (Karwan):** Don't try to force yourself to like it. Recognize that the "not liking" is a state of mind you carry. Get into seclusion, cultivate happiness *first*, and then perhaps return to the job with a new mind—or realize you don't need that job anymore.
- **On Exposure vs. Avoidance (Catherine):** If you lack skill, **avoid** triggers (news, Gucci stores) to get your mind happy first. Once skilled, you can look at them without craving.

### Final Takeaway
The core teaching is that **suffering is wanting something you don't have**. The entire practice is learning to see that craving (Tanha) in real-time, and choosing to be satisfied right now rather than trying to become something else.

### Metaphors and Stories

This talk by Dhammarato is rich with metaphors and stories to explain the **Second Noble Truth** (the cause of suffering) and how to practice with it. Here are the key metaphors and stories used:

### 1. The Metaphor of "Thirst" (Tanha)
- **Central Metaphor:** The Pali word *Tanha* literally means **"thirst."** Dhammarato uses this to illustrate the difference between a manageable desire and a desperate, suffering-inducing one.
    - **Example:** "Imagine you're at your desk and get thirsty. You take a drink. Easy peasy."
    - **Contrast:** "Now pretend you're in the middle of the Sahara Desert with an empty canteen... The thirst gets ferocious. Mostly the ferocious part is mental."

### 2. The Story of Samatha and the Khathin Ceremony
- **Story:** Young Samatha (before becoming an Arahant) attends a ceremony with Ajahn Chah. All the young women are dressed in their finest. Samatha is "getting an eyeful." Ajahn Chah nudges him and asks, "Well, what do you think?"
    - **Samatha's Answer:** "I like it, but I don't want it."
    - **Teaching Point:** This story illustrates the key difference between **Vedana** (feeling: liking) and **Tanha** (craving: wanting). Samatha could see the pleasant feeling without allowing it to escalate into desire and suffering.

### 3. The Two Arrows (or Two Darts)
- **Metaphor:** The Buddha speaks of two kinds of arrows or darts.
    - **First Dart:** Physical pain or bodily sensation (e.g., a knee hurting, old age, sickness). This is unavoidable.
    - **Second Dart:** The mental reaction to that pain: "I don't like it," frustration, wanting to get rid of it (Vibhava Tanha). This is optional and often more painful than the first.
    - **Teaching Point:** You can't always avoid the first arrow, but you can learn to not shoot the second one. "No need for that second arrow."

### 4. The Cliff and the Edge
- **Metaphor:** "Let us say that there's a cliff right in front of you. And that you start walking towards that cliff... if you continue to walk towards that cliff, then you're going to go over the side."
    - **Teaching Point:** Tanha (wanting) is the **direction** of walking toward the cliff. Upadana (clinging) is actually **falling over** the edge. The practice is to stop walking before you fall. You can see the edge and choose to turn back.

### 5. The Hot Coal
- **Metaphor:** "If you've got a really hot coal and you don't like carrying it, set it down, dress your hand, and become happy. And then don't pick up that coal again."
    - **Teaching Point:** This is for Karwan, who struggles with his job. The advice isn't to "like" the coal; it's to **drop the mental state** of not-liking (the hot coal) before you try to do anything else.

### 6. The Purses (Geraldine and Catherine)
- **Story:** "There Geraldine is walking down the street... She passes a dress shop and she sees a purse, and now she wants that purse."
    - **Contrast with Catherine:** Dhammarato asks Catherine how many purses she has. She says "none" and doesn't need a Gucci.
    - **Teaching Point:** The seeing and liking of the purse is not the problem. The **wanting** is the problem (Tanha). The practice is to see the purse, like it, and then *not want it*.

### 7. The Metaphor of Good Eyesight (20/20 Vision)
- **Metaphor:** "Some of you here have 20/20 eyesight... you can see well... we can gain pleasure from being able to see."
    - **Teaching Point:** Seeing Dukkha (dissatisfaction) should be treated the same way. Instead of wanting to get rid of it when you see it, you should congratulate yourself: *"Aha, I see you."* You have the "good eyesight" to recognize suffering. That is a cause for joy, not for more wanting.

### 8. The Woeful States (Four Metaphors for Mental States)
Dhammarato explains the four woeful states as living metaphors for common human experiences:
- **Hell:** Being in the heat of an argument or feeling trapped. "If you're in the heat of an argument, you're in hell."
- **Hungry Ghost (Preta):** Wanting something you can't get, like standing at a window wanting a purse. "A great big belly and a little tiny mouth."
- **Dumb Animal:** Being forced to do something you don't like, every day (like a job or school). "If you've got a job and you don't like it, that means you're a dumb animal."
- **Titan (Asura):** Fear and trepidation, wanting to avoid something. "All dressed up and no place to go... playing hooky to avoid school."

### 9. The Two Paths of Practice (Goenka vs. Bhikkhu Buddhadasa)
- **Story/Comparison:** A student on a Goenka retreat sits with painful legs and is told to sit still with strong determination. A student with Bhikkhu Buddhadasa is told to "move your legs around, stand up, walk around."
    - **Teaching Point:** The Buddha's path is not about enduring unnecessary pain to "gain" something. It's about **doing what you can to eliminate discomfort right now**. "If you can do something about it, do something about it. And if you can't do anything about it, at least you can enjoy it."

### 10. The Seclusion of the Mind (The Empty Room)
- **Metaphor/Advice for Catherine:** "You can close the door to your bedroom... In this closed room there are no jobs, there are no alligators, there's no Gucci bags... and I'm happy that that's the case."
    - **Teaching Point:** True seclusion isn't just getting away from external objects; it's getting the mind to stop generating the story of not-liking. You practice being happy with *nothing* so that you can then carry that happiness back into the world.

### 11. The Insomnia Story (Advice for Tom)
- **Story:** Tom is struggling with insomnia, trying to force himself to sleep, leading to frustration.
    - **Dhammarato's Advice:** Drop the rule that you "must" sleep. Instead, enjoy being awake. "Oh, I'm so happy I don't have to sleep right now. Oh, I'm so happy I can just lay here and feel good."
    - **Teaching Point:** The suffering comes from the *rule* (the ideal of what should happen). Let go of the rule, and the war in the mind ceases.
