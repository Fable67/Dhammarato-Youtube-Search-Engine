### Summary

Here is a summary of the Dhamma talk with Dhammarato and Sangha friends.

### Core Teaching: Atamayata (Not Making Things Up / Not "I, Me, Mine")

The talk centers on the Pali word **Atamayata** (or Atamayo). While Bhikkhu Buddhadasa used it similarly to "Arahant" (no delusion), Dhammarato clarifies its simpler, more practical meaning: **not making stuff up**. It means not attributing a self ("I," "me," "mine") to experiences, states, or objects. The "devil made me do it" is a metaphor for when our own desires and self-centered thoughts drive us, but the real solution is to see that we are actively creating that suffering through attachment.

**The Progression of Practice:**
1.  **Broad Attachments:** It begins with not identifying with large, external things like your country, political party, or even your high school band. When you identify with these, you suffer when they fluctuate or fail.
2.  **Specific Attachments:** The practice deepens to not personalizing even good states of mind, like the "wow" feeling of early success. As you get better, it becomes ordinary. Even being a Dhamma teacher is just a role; the teaching belongs to the Buddha and is not "mine."
3.  **Not Identifying with Perception (Anatta-Sanna):** This is the key to clear seeing. When you take the "self" out of perception, you remove confirmation bias. You can see things, people, and events as they are, not through the lens of "how it affects me" or "what I want."
4.  **The Result:** With a clear, unbiased view, you can see cause and effect (causality) more clearly. This leads to a state of well-being because suffering (dukkha) comes from an "I" that wants things to be different. When there is no "I" attached to the outcome, there is no suffering.

**The Three Suttas Referenced:**
- **Sutta 113** (Good in the Middle): Even in high states like Jhana, do not identify as "I am in Jhana." A true person is not attached to mind states.
- **Sutta 137** (Good in the End): Do not identify with the six internal sense bases (including the mind).
- **The Atamayata Sutta**: (Regarding the last three points) If there is no "I" in perception, our knowledge becomes noble and clear, free from superstitious or biased thinking, and we understand causality.

### Q&A and Discussion Highlights:

**1. On Doubt and Letting Go (for Dan):**
- Dan said he wants to "get rid" of the "I" but often sees it only in hindsight.
- **Dhammarato:** The desire to "get rid of it" is a new "I" that is dwelling in dukkha. The practice is not to fight it, but to **see it clearly** in the moment. See the "I" that is trying to get rid of the "I," recognize it as a made-up story, and drop it.

**2. On Caring and Worldly Problems (for Manuela):**
- Manuela felt the teaching "I don't care" about the situation in Iran was harsh, as she is an active, caring person.
- **Dhammarato:** The "caring" that causes suffering is a selfish caring, which is really an attachment to wanting things to be my way. When you care about a conflict on the other side of the world, you are adding another finger pushing on that wagon, crushing it. All parties care about their version of the outcome, and that is what causes the conflict. Backing off personally doesn't mean you don't provide help to those you know and can influence; it means you stop letting the world's problems control your inner peace.

**3. On Chronic Pain and Blame (for Tammy):**
- Tammy has inflammation and is worried it signals deeper damage, and she blames herself ("I must be doing something wrong").
- **Dhammarato:** First, the worry and self-blame are the real unhealthy states. Since she has followed her doctor's advice, she can tell that worried "parent mind" to relax. Pain is a message, not an enemy. Don't kill the messenger. The body will get old, sick, and die; the question is, "Can you do it happily?"
- The biggest point: **It doesn't matter if the pain goes away.** The health of the mind is what matters. She is actively engaging in unwholesome thoughts right now by calling it "unbearable" and blaming herself for it. He advises her to cease that mental activity and practice "Don't worry, be happy." This is the **core teaching** to apply not just to her, but to everyone on the call.

### Final Summary
The talk emphasizes that **all suffering is self-created** through the process of making things personal. The path is not about achieving a state but about ending the process of attributing self. The way to be happy is to **stop worrying about it and stop making it about you**. The entire discussion synthesizes into the practical guidance: **Don't worry, be happy.**

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the talk, here are the key metaphors and stories used by Speaker A (Dhammarato) to explain the concept of **Atamayata** (not making things up, not identifying with or owning experiences):

**1. The "Devil Made Me Do It" (Flip Wilson)**
- **Metaphor:** The devil represents one's own desires and deluded mind, not an external being.
- **Story:** Dhammarato describes a 1960s comedy skit by Flip Wilson. In the skit, Wilson (as a cross-dresser) claims the devil made him buy and wear a dress, despite his initial resistance. The humor lies in the audience understanding that the "devil" is actually his own inner desires, not a supernatural force. This illustrates how we blame external forces ("the devil") for our own mental fabrications, and how identifying with desires leads to suffering.

**2. The "Making Stuff Up" Metaphor**
- **Metaphor:** Atamayata is defined as "not making stuff up." The mind's habit of creating narratives, identities, and attachments is "making stuff up." This is used to emphasize that suffering arises from our own mental construction of a self and ownership.

**3. The Musical Instrument Analogy**
- **Metaphor:** Mastery and non-attachment to skill.
- **Story:** A beginner feels immense pride and joy ("wow feeling") after playing their first piece of music. However, after 20 years of practice, that same piece becomes ordinary and no longer brings the same thrill. This shows how initial accomplishments are seen as "mine" and a source of ego, but as one progresses, these states become less important to the self. The point is that even spiritual accomplishments (like Jhana) should not be clung to as "mine."

**4. The "This Is My Country" Story**
- **Metaphor:** Ownership and identity with external groups (country, band, church).
- **Story:** Dhammarato recalls playing a song called "This Is My Country" in his high school band. He realized, even then, that he didn't own the country. He extends this to his experiences with the football team, the band, and the church. He was *in* the band and *in* the church, but he did not identify with them as "mine." This illustrates the concept of being in the world but not of it, not letting group identities define or cause suffering.

**5. The Farmer's Wagon in the Creek**
- **Metaphor:** The harm of excessive, selfish "caring" or attachment to outcomes.
- **Story:** A farmer's wagon rolls into a creek. The whole town tries to help, but so many people care and push in opposite directions (north, south, upstream, downstream) that the wagon is crushed under the weight of their conflicting efforts. This is used to explain why "caring" about distant political conflicts (like Iran) is unskillful. Everyone's selfish attachment to their own solution creates more chaos and suffering, rather than solving the problem.

**6. The "Pain as a Messenger" Metaphor**
- **Metaphor:** Pain is a messenger from the body, not an enemy to be killed.
- **Story:** Dhammarato explains that people often "kill the messenger" (the body's pain) because they don't like the message (that something is wrong or that the body is aging). This is used in the conversation with Tammy, who is anxious about her pain. He suggests she listen to the message practically (follow doctor's orders) but not let it create anxiety or self-blame, as the mind's reaction to pain is what creates the suffering, not the pain itself.
