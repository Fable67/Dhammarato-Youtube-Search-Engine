### Summary

This Dhamma talk, led by Dhammarato (Speaker A) with Sangha friends Kishon (Speaker B) and Arthur (Speaker C), centers on a core insight: **the feeling of delight (pīti) arises from within, not from external objects.** The discussion uses a personal story as its springboard and expands into a practical meditation guide.

### **Core Summary**

The talk explores the moment of "delight" or "thrill" that arises when seeing something attractive (e.g., a person, a product). The key teaching is that **this delight is an internal, fleeting feeling mistakenly projected onto an external object.** By learning to observe, understand, and skillfully work with this feeling, we can transform our practice and daily life.

### **Main Themes & Teachings**

1.  **"I like it, but I don't want it":** This phrase, attributed to Ajahn Sumedho, is presented as a crucial tool. It allows one to acknowledge the initial feeling of delight without letting it develop into craving, clinging, or unwise action. It creates a gap between sensation and reaction.

2.  **The Mistake of Projection:** We habitually believe external things (a person, a car) *cause* our delight. The truth is the delight is generated internally. The proof is that the same object doesn't cause delight in everyone. This mistaken belief leads to suffering when we chase external objects to re-create an internal feeling.

3.  **Delight as a Skill & Practice:** The feeling of delight (pīti) is not just for rare moments. It can be cultivated as a skill.
    *   **Observe it:** Notice these fleeting moments of delight throughout the day—enjoying food, seeing something beautiful.
    *   **Recall it:** Use wholesome childhood memories (e.g., the joy of running into a park) to access and regenerate the *feeling* of delight, not the memory's story.
    *   **Cultivate it:** Actively use these memories and observations during meditation (ānāpānasati) to "gladden the mind," nurture oneself, and counteract the default mode of critical thinking.

4.  **From Critical Mind to Nurturing Mind:** Our default state is often a "critical mind" (judging good/bad, liking/disliking), which poisons our experience—analogous to the Biblical "Fall" via the fruit of the knowledge of good and evil. The path is to develop a "nurturing mind," which is friendly, joyful, and accepting.

5.  **The Spectrum of Relationship:** The talk outlines a progression in how we relate to experience:
    *   Hot War → Cold War → **Tolerance** → **Acceptance** (still involves dukkha) → **Friendship** (metta, unity) → **Delight in "What Is" (Amor Fati)**.
    *   The highest aim is *amor fati* (love of fate): delighting in and falling in love with the entire cycle of change (anicca), not just the pleasant parts.

6.  **Wisdom through Joy, Not Just Critique:** Insight into anicca (impermanence), dukkha (unsatisfactoriness), and anattā (not-self) can be approached not only through analytical critique but also through the **absence** of dukkha and the **delight** in selflessness. "Look for the absence of dukkha. Hey, there's no dukkha here now."

### **Practical Instructions Given**

*   **To-Do List:** Start watching for instantaneous delights throughout your day.
*   **Meditation Practice:** Use the memory of a *wholesome* delightful feeling (like childhood joy) to regenerate that feeling during sitting. Stay with the feeling itself, not the story around it.
*   **Reframe:** When delight arises, see it as a "wake-up call" to practice, not as a signal to chase an object.
*   **Shift Default Mode:** Practice moving from a critical, problem-focused default state to a delighted, "everything is okay" default state.

### **Conclusion**

The talk reframes the spiritual path. Instead of being a grim battle against desire, it is a joyful practice of **reclaiming our innate capacity for delight** by understanding its true, internal source. By learning to generate and sustain wholesome delight (pīti and sukha), we overcome the hindrances, gain insight into reality, and naturally move from a critical, suffering mind to a friendly, delighted one that embraces the whole flow of life.

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the transcript, the talk uses several key **metaphors and stories** to illustrate its points about desire, delight, and mindfulness:

### **Core Metaphors & Analogies:**

1.  **"I like it, but I don't want it."**
    *   This is the central, repeated phrase. It's presented as a crucial tool for creating a gap between an initial feeling of delight/sensation and the subsequent craving or action. It's a way to acknowledge a feeling without being compelled by it.

2.  **The "Thrill" or "Delight" as a Fleeting Sensation:**
    *   This feeling is compared to a spark or a quick wave that arises and passes. The talk emphasizes that it is **internally generated**, not caused by the external object (a person, a car, etc.). This is a core metaphor for understanding the mind's role in creating experience.

3.  **The "Magic Trick" or "Delusion" of Projection:**
    *   The speaker describes the mistake of believing the external object (the girl, the car) *caused* the delight, and therefore projecting a future of continuous delight onto it. This is likened to playing a trick on oneself: "every time that I can see that girl, I'll feel that way. Guess what?"

4.  **The "Internal Created Image" (Saṇyāsaṇa/ Saññā):**
    *   After the initial sensation, the mind creates a mental image or representation of the object to try to rekindle the delight. The speaker contrasts this mental fabrication with the "real girl," highlighting how desire is often for a mental projection, not reality.

5.  **The "Wave" of Dukkha (Suffering/Unsatisfactoriness):**
    *   Dukkha is not described as a constant low hum, but as a wave with peaks and valleys. The practice involves recognizing not just the peaks of suffering, but also the **valleys where there is "no Dukkha at all."** This reframes the goal from eliminating a constant to understanding a fluctuating pattern.

6.  **The "Uphill and Downhill" Cycle:**
    *   Life and sensation are compared to a cycle of climbing a hill (effort, potential struggle) and coasting downhill (ease, delight). The talk suggests falling in love with the *entire cycle* of change (anicca) rather than clinging to only the "downhill" parts.

### **Key Stories & Anecdotes:**

1.  **The 7-Eleven Story (Kishon's Anecdote):**
    *   This is the triggering story. Kishon sees a "delightful looking girl" in a store, feels the initial "thrill," but uses the "I like it, but I don't want it" teaching to observe the feeling and the potential dangers of acting on it, letting it pass.

2.  **The Ajahn Chah and Ajahn Sumedho Story:**
    *   This is the origin story of the phrase "I like it, but I don't want it." At a ceremony with many adorned young women, Ajahn Chah notices Ajahn Sumedho's delight. When asked about it, Sumedho admits, "I do like it, but I don't want it." This story legitimizes the phrase and shows its application by a respected teacher.

3.  **The Story of Adam and Eve (Bhikkhu Buddhadasa's Interpretation):**
    *   This is used as a **deep metaphor for the critical mind**. Eating the fruit from the "tree of the knowledge of good and evil" is interpreted as adopting a **critical, judgmental mindset** ("this is good, this is bad"). This mindset is what poisons our natural "paradise" (Garden of Eden) and creates dukkha. The "fruit" is the poisoned result of that critical thinking.

4.  **The Child's Delight in the Park (Kishon's Memory):**
    *   Kishon recalls the pure, uncomplicated delight of running into a park as a child. This becomes a **benchmark for wholesome delight**—a feeling not tied to sensual desire but to freedom, play, and simple joy. It's offered as a memory to "gladden the mind" in meditation.

5.  **The "Half Sutta" (Saṃyutta Nikaya reference):**
    *   The speaker references a sutta where Venerable Sāriputta says friendship is "half the holy life," but the Buddha corrects him, saying it is the *whole* of the holy life. This story underscores the importance of **friendship (mettā)**—first with oneself (ending self-criticism), then with the world—as the foundation of practice.

6.  **The Lime Ice Cream Memory (Speaker A's Childhood):**
    *   The speaker recalls the specific delight of getting lime ice cream from a local store as a child. Like the park memory, this is used as an example of a **pure feeling of delight** that can be recalled and used to generate wholesome joy in the present, separate from the object (the ice cream) itself.

7.  **The Red Cross Analogy:**
    *   Used to illustrate the concept of "self-preservation" losing the original purpose. The Red Cross, initially about donating blood, now focuses largely on fundraising to sustain itself. This metaphor extends to the human tendency to shift from a joyful, nurturing phase of life to a critical, self-preserving phase, where we cling to life without enjoying it.

### **Foreign/Learned Terms Used as Conceptual Metaphors:**

*   **Pīti:** Translated as "rapture" or "joy," this is the Pali term for the elevated feeling of delight being discussed.
*   **Amor Fati (Latin):** Translated as "love of one's fate." This is presented as the ideal attitude: a delighted, joyful acceptance of whatever happens as part of the cyclical nature of reality, beyond mere tolerance or acceptance.
*   **Saṇyāsaṇa (Pali):** Referred to as the "internal representation" or mental fabrication created after sensory contact, which we then cling to.

In summary, the talk uses a blend of personal anecdotes, Buddhist teaching stories, and modern analogies to build a central argument: that a fleeting, internally-generated feeling of "delight" is often mistaken as coming from external objects, leading to craving and suffering. The path out is to recognize this process, use the phrase "I like it, but I don't want it" to create space, and consciously cultivate wholesome delight and a friendly, non-critical mind (*amor fati*) through mindfulness and memory.
