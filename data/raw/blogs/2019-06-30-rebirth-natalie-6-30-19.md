### Summary

This is a rich and wide-ranging Dhamma conversation between Dhammarato (Speaker A) and a student (Speaker B). Here is a summary of the key themes and teachings:

**1. The Airport Scene: Spiritual Materialism & Devotion**
Speaker B describes seeing a crowd of Buddhists excitedly rushing to greet a high Lama at the airport, pushing to be first in line for a blessed scarf. Speaker A (Dhammarato) frames this as **"spiritual materialism"** —treating the spiritual path as an external, transactional affair (seeking blessings, belonging, and magical protection) rather than an internal transformation. He notes that this devotion to an external guru or ceremony is a form of delusion, where the teacher becomes the object of the student’s unexamined desires.

**2. The History of Kamma & Rebirth: From Magical Thinking to Responsibility**
Dhammarato gives a detailed historical analysis of how the concept of *Kamma* (karma) was corrupted:
- **Original Buddhist view:** Kamma is simply cause and effect operating in the present moment (e.g., I smile, you smile back).
- **Brahminical corruption:** Around 300 BC, under King Asoka, the teaching was co-opted. Kamma became a cosmic, moralistic "machine" that stores good and bad deeds across lifetimes to justify social hierarchy (caste) and to serve the human need for revenge and blame.
- **The real meaning:** The Buddha taught that suffering is created *in this moment* by our own minds. We cannot blame a past-life Kamma machine or an outside force. The true teaching is **100% personal responsibility.**

**3. The Nature of Consciousness & "No Rebirth"**
Dhammarato explains that the Buddha rejected the idea of a permanent soul or consciousness that reincarnates. Consciousness is *dependently arisen* (like a fire depending on its fuel) and changes moment to moment. Therefore, there is nothing substantial enough to "survive" death and be reborn.
- **The "Code":** He suggests that terms like "rebirth" in the suttas are often coded language, used to communicate with a magical-thinking audience. The real teaching is about seeing that there is no self to be reborn.
- **"Lifetimes" as Moments:** A "lifetime" can be understood as the birth and death of a self-view from one moment to the next. This reframes the idea of "sending metta for the next life" as simply wishing well for the next moment.

**4. The Two Streams: Noble vs. Religious Buddhism**
Dhammarato distinguishes between two parallel traditions:
- **The Buddhist Religion (Ignoble):** Focuses on rituals, blessings, rules, magical thinking, and devotion to a guru. It keeps people in a child-like state of blame and hope for external salvation.
- **The Noble Dhamma (Ariya Dhamma):** The hidden, esoteric teaching of the Buddha. It is about self-responsibility, seeing things as they are, and cleaning up one's own mind *now*.

**5. Bhikkhu Buddhadasa & The "Outing" of the Noble Dhamma**
Dhammarato explains that **Bhikkhu Buddhadasa** was a key figure who, in the 1930s, began publicly teaching the Noble Dhamma that had been kept secret within the Sangha. He was criticized for teaching it to "the wrong people" (the laity).
- **The "Dual Audience" Technique:** Most teachers (like the Dalai Lama or Goenka) use a dual approach—speaking magically to the immature audience and nobly to the mature audience.
- **The New Mandate:** Dhammarato, following Buddhadasa and Achan Po, believes that the Noble Dhamma no longer needs to be hidden. The time is ripe to bring it out into the open, especially to the West, which has received a watered-down, commercialized version of Buddhism.

**6. The Student's Doubt & Resolution**
Speaker B's original confusion was resolved: why do noble teachers (like Goenka) talk about rebirth and karma? Dhammarato’s answer is that they are often speaking to a **dual audience**—using conventional language to benefit those not yet ready for the deeper truth. The true teaching is about taking full responsibility for one's own mind in the present moment, without blaming a cosmic Kamma machine.

**In short: The talk is a call to grow up spiritually—to move from a child-like, magical religion of blame and external blessings to a mature, noble Dhamma of total self-responsibility and liberation in the here and now.**

### Metaphors and Stories

Based on the talk, here are the key metaphors and stories used by Speaker A:

### Metaphors

1.  **The Fairy Tale or Children's Story:**
    - **Used for:** Rebirth, karma as a cosmic justice system, God.
    - **How:** Speaker A repeatedly refers to beliefs in rebirth, karma (as a long-term moral accounting system), and God as "children's stories" or "magical thinking" designed to keep children in line. He contrasts this with "growing up" and taking responsibility.
    - **Key Quote:** "we do not have to hold beliefs about the past or the future, because these are children's stories that are designed to keep children in line."

2.  **The Projector / Film:**
    - **Used for:** The nature of consciousness.
    - **How:** He compares consciousness to a film projector that is only "on" half the time, turning on and off rapidly, to argue that consciousness is fleeting and impermanent, not a solid entity that could travel from one life to another.
    - **Key Quote:** "It's very much like the film... the projector... it would only be on half the time, and then it would go off... The mind is also very much like that."

3.  **The Stairs:**
    - **Used for:** The spectrum of beliefs about life after death.
    - **How:** He uses a descending staircase as a metaphor for a hierarchy of views, from the most solid (reincarnation/Christian soul) at the top, down through rebirth, to atheism, and finally to the "actual teaching of the Buddha" at the bottom, which he calls "ain't nothing there."
    - **Key Quote:** "imagine it's like this, like a set of stairs, and up here is reincarnation... The Buddha's stuff is way, way down here. Ain't nothing there."

4.  **Salt and Pepper / Condiment:**
    - **Used for:** The infiltration of Brahmanical beliefs into Buddhism.
    - **How:** He describes how Brahmanism has been mixed into the Buddhist religion like a "condiment" or "salt and peppered" into it, changing the flavor of the original teachings.
    - **Key Quote:** "that the Buddhist religion has covered over that... it has... a condiment or salt and peppered by Brahmanism."

5.  **The Foxhole:**
    - **Used for:** The fear of death and the rejection of magical thinking (especially by atheists).
    - **How:** He subverts the Christian adage "There are no atheists in foxholes" by arguing that atheists are too smart to be in foxholes in the first place, implying that rejecting magical belief systems requires intelligence and non-conformity.
    - **Key Quote:** "There are no atheists in foxholes because the atheists were too smart to fall for the platter that says, come join us, be a patriot and join the army."

6.  **The Computer / Hard Drive / AI:**
    - **Used for:** The absurd complexity of the law of karma.
    - **How:** He uses a computer science analogy (hard drives, computing power, AI) to demonstrate the practical impossibility of a cosmic karma machine tracking and processing every action across countless lifetimes, concluding it's "a bunch of bull."
    - **Key Quote:** "how much horsepower of computing you have for observation... how many hard drives it would take to store the data... it winds up being an enormous exercise... it's a bunch of bull."

### Stories

1.  **The Story of the Airport Lama (from Speaker B):**
    - **Context:** Speaker B describes seeing a crowd of devotees rush to greet a high Lama at the airport, fighting to be first to have their white scarves blessed.
    - **Speaker A's Use:** Speaker A immediately labels this as "spiritual materialism," a story about people turning a teacher into an "object of delusion" and seeking external salvation, which he contrasts with the internal work of the authentic teaching.

2.  **The Story of King Ashoka:**
    - **Context:** A historical story about the emperor Ashoka in 300 BC.
    - **Speaker A's Use:** Speaker A claims Ashoka invented the "Buddhist religion" (as opposed to the Buddha's teaching) by giving royal patronage to the Sangha. This led to a mass influx of monks who were not interested in the Dhamma but in the patronage, overwhelming the true practitioners and allowing Brahmanical beliefs (like karma and rebirth) to infiltrate Buddhism.

3.  **The Story of the 14-Year-Old and the Revenge:**
    - **Context:** A hypothetical story about a boy who sees his father shot in a bar, and seven years later kills the shooter.
    - **Speaker A's Use:** He uses this to debunk the idea of a cosmic "karma machine" that tracks actions over lifetimes. Instead, he explains the cause-and-effect is immediate and psychological: the boy *kept the memory and desire for revenge alive in his own mind* for seven years, leading to the second shooting. It was his own mental activity, not a machine.

4.  **The Story of David and the Sri Lankan Abbot:**
    - **Context:** A personal anecdote about a friend (David) who overheard a Sri Lankan abbot in Kuala Lumpur telling a Chinese woman that her husband was in heaven.
    - **Speaker A's Use:** This story illustrates the "dual audience" teaching method. The abbot gave "magical" consolation to the layperson, but when David said he was from Wat Suan Mokkh (a center for the "Noble Dharma"), the abbot's whole demeanor changed and he immediately began teaching the real, non-magical Dhamma.

5.  **The Story of Bhikkhu Buddhadasa's Summons:**
    - **Context:** An historical event from the 1930s.
    - **Speaker A's Use:** Speaker A tells how Bhikkhu Buddhadasa was summoned to a council in Bangkok for teaching the "Noble Dharma" publicly. The council agreed his teachings were correct but decided they should be kept within the Sangha, not taught to the public. This story is used to explain why the true Dhamma has been hidden.

6.  **The Story of a Noble Teacher in the Soviet Union:**
    - **Context:** A story from Speaker B about a Buddhist teacher in the Soviet Union.
    - **Speaker A's Use:** Speaker A uses the teacher's two responses as a perfect example of the dual-audience approach. When asked about healing water, the teacher gave a literal, physical answer ("it's great for thirst"). When asked for an astrological wedding date, he said "any day is a good day" but then did a calculation, showing he could speak both the "noble" truth and the "magical" language of his audience to appease them without lying.
